# Fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum) — Traditional Uses

## Ayurvedic Medicine (Methi)

Fenugreek is known as **Methi** in the Ayurvedic tradition and is included in the **Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API)**.

### Traditional Applications (per API monograph)
- **Digestive tonic and appetite stimulant** (Deepana, Pachana) — one of its primary traditional roles, stimulating digestive fire (agni)
- **Rejuvenative** (Rasayana) — classified among tonics that support vitality and longevity
- **Support for diabetes and hyperlipidemia** — traditional use that has been substantiated by modern clinical evidence (Neelakantan 2014 meta-analysis: fasting glucose -0.96 mmol/L, HbA1c -0.85%)
- **Galactagogue** — promotes breast milk production; one of the most widely known traditional uses globally, supported by clinical data (Khan 2018, n=78)
- **Used for low libido and seminal debility** — the aphrodisiac and reproductive tonic use that modern testosterone research investigates

### Ayurvedic Context
The seed is the primary medicinal part. Traditional preparations include powdered seed, decoctions, and incorporation into food. The warming, slightly bitter quality of fenugreek aligns with its classification as a digestive and metabolic herb in Ayurveda.

## European Pharmacopoeia Traditions

### German Commission E Monograph
- **Status:** Official monograph
- **Plant part:** Semen (seed)
- **Approved uses:**
  - Internal use for **loss of appetite**
  - External use as a **poultice for local inflammation**
- **Daily dose:** 6 grams of drug, or equivalent preparations
- **Note:** The Commission E approval is specifically for appetite stimulation and topical anti-inflammatory use — no hormonal claims.

### EMA (European Medicines Agency) Herbal Monograph
- **Status:** Monograph available
- **Plant part:** Semen (seed)
- **Traditional indications recognized:**
  - Temporary loss of appetite
  - Symptomatic treatment of minor skin inflammations (external use)
- **Regulatory position:** Recognized for traditional use in the EU, but **not for hormonal claims**
- The EMA assessment explicitly limits the recognized scope to appetite and topical use, reflecting the conservative regulatory stance on the hormonal evidence.

## Middle Eastern & Mediterranean Traditions

Fenugreek has deep roots in Middle Eastern, North African, and Mediterranean cultures where it functions as both food and medicine:

- **Culinary use:** Seeds and leaves are staple ingredients in Middle Eastern, Indian, Ethiopian, and North African cuisines. The seeds are used whole, sprouted, or ground in spice blends. Fresh and dried leaves (kasuri methi) are used as herbs.
- **Medicinal food tradition:** The boundary between culinary and medicinal use is fluid — fenugreek-enriched foods are consumed both for flavor and for their perceived health benefits (digestion, strength, nursing support).
- **Historical depth:** One of the oldest cultivated medicinal plants, with records of use in ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome. Referenced in the Ebers Papyrus.

## Cross-Cultural Convergence

The traditional use patterns are remarkably consistent across cultures:

| Use | Ayurveda | Commission E | EMA | Middle Eastern |
|-----|----------|-------------|-----|----------------|
| Appetite / digestive | Yes (Deepana, Pachana) | Approved | Recognized | Yes |
| Galactagogue | Yes | — | — | Yes |
| Aphrodisiac / reproductive | Yes | — | — | Yes |
| Topical inflammation | — | Approved | Recognized | Yes |
| Blood sugar support | Yes | — | — | Yes |

The appetite and digestive uses have the broadest regulatory recognition. The reproductive/aphrodisiac use is widespread in traditional practice but not recognized by European regulatory bodies.
