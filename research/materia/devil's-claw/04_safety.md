# Devil's Claw - Safety

**Species:** *Harpagophytum procumbens* DC. (primary); *H. zeyheri* Decne. (secondary)

---

## Overall Safety Profile

**Vlachojannis 2008 systematic safety review** (28 trials, 20 with AE data, PMID 18236448):
- Minor adverse events in approximately **3% of patients**, mainly gastrointestinal
- In **none** of the double-blind studies was AE incidence during Harpagophytum treatment higher than placebo
- No drug interactions documented in the review
- A few acute toxicity reports; no chronic toxicity documented
- Conclusion: preparations likely safe; additional long-term data urgently needed

**Comparison to NSAIDs (Chrubasik 2003, PMID 12509627):**
- Doloteffin (60 mg harpagoside/day) vs. rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day: Drop-outs due to AEs = **1 (Doloteffin) vs. 6 (rofecoxib)**
- GI complaints were more severe in the rofecoxib group

---

## Common Adverse Effects

### Gastrointestinal (Most Common)
- Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
- Reported in ~3% of patients in surveillance data
- Diarrhea in 8.1% receiving Harpadol vs. 26.7% receiving diacerhein (Chantre 2000)
- Dyspeptic complaints and sensation of fullness (Wegener 2003: 2 cases in n=75)
- Increased gastric acid secretion possible (mechanism: bitter principle stimulation)

### Headache and Vertigo
- Listed in EMA monograph as potential adverse effects
- Low incidence in clinical trials

### Minor Drug Reactions (Long-Term Use)
- Three patients experienced minor drug reactions over 21,761 patient-days (54-week follow-up, Chrubasik 2005)
- No serious reactions in long-term open-label study

---

## Contraindications

### Gastric or Duodenal Ulcers
- **Contraindicated** (EMA HMPC 2016)
- Mechanism: Devil's claw root acts as a bitter digestive stimulant, increasing gastric acid secretion; this can exacerbate peptic ulcer disease
- Evidence level: Pharmacological reasoning + regulatory precaution (not from clinical AE reports)

### Pregnancy
- **Contraindicated** (EMA HMPC 2016)
- Mechanism: Oxytocic properties reported in vitro for *H. procumbens* extracts; theoretical risk of stimulating uterine contractions
- Evidence level: In vitro data only; no human AE reports confirming adverse pregnancy outcomes
- Precautionary principle applies given potential risk; not suitable for use in pregnancy

### Known Hypersensitivity
- Contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to Harpagophytum preparations
- Hypersensitivity reactions listed: rash, hives, face swelling (EMA HMPC 2016)

### Gallstones
- Caution advised (Alt Med Rev 2008 monograph)
- Mechanism: Bile stimulation; potential to trigger biliary colic in patients with existing gallstones
- Not a formal EMA contraindication but flagged in the Natural Standard/Alt Med Rev review

---

## Drug Interactions

### Anticoagulants (Warfarin)
- **Theoretical concern** — several case reports suggest possible potentiation of anticoagulant activity
- Mechanism hypothesized: modulation of CYP450 enzyme activity affecting warfarin metabolism
- **Clinical implication:** Monitor INR in patients taking warfarin who begin devil's claw supplementation
- Evidence level: Case reports and theoretical; no controlled pharmacokinetic interaction studies
- Vlachojannis 2008: No drug interactions documented in 28-trial systematic review — but this review may not have been powered to detect rare interactions

### Antidiabetic Medications
- Potential for hypoglycemic effect (traditional use for diabetes; some preclinical data)
- Theoretical interaction with insulin or oral hypoglycemics; blood glucose monitoring advised
- Evidence level: Preclinical and traditional use data only

### Antihypertensive Medications
- Context: One case report of hypertension induced by devil's claw (Cuspidi 2015 — see below)
- **Theoretical concern in either direction:** Proposed COX-2-mediated vasoconstrictive mechanism could raise blood pressure, potentially antagonizing antihypertensives
- Evidence level: Single case report

### Cardiac Medications (Arrhythmia Drugs)
- Some references suggest potential effects on cardiac conduction at high doses
- Mentioned in Alt Med Rev 2008 monograph
- Evidence level: Theoretical / preclinical; no clinical case reports

---

## Notable Adverse Event Case Reports

### Hypertension — Cuspidi 2015 (PMID 26094951)
- **Patient:** 62-year-old postmenopausal woman; no prior hypertension history
- **Dose:** Two caplets/day (250 mg each = 500 mg/day) for hand osteoarthritis
- **Duration:** 2 weeks of use
- **Adverse effect:** Grade 2 symptomatic hypertension; BP reaching **175/100 mmHg** with headache and dizziness
- **Resolution:** BP normalized within 2 weeks of discontinuation; ambulatory average 124/72 mmHg post-discontinuation
- **Proposed mechanism:** Harpagoside COX-2 inhibition → suppression of vasodilator prostaglandins → increased vasoconstriction (analogous to NSAID-class hypertensive mechanism)
- **Clinical significance:** First documented case of hypertension associated with *H. procumbens* in humans; warrants pharmacovigilance caution especially in hypertensive patients or those at cardiovascular risk

## Toxicology

### Animal Safety (High-Dose)
- **High doses (600 mg/kg body weight) caused fetal kidney necrosis** in animal models (Gxaba 2022, PMID 35684573)
- This dose dramatically exceeds any human therapeutic exposure
- No chronic animal toxicity documented at therapeutic dose ranges

### Long-Term Human Data
- Only one dataset: 21,761 patient-days (Chrubasik 2005); open-label; no placebo; three minor reactions
- No formal 12-month placebo-controlled safety study has been conducted
- Bargsten 2025: Long-term safety data "urgently needed" — regulatory gap acknowledged

### Pediatric Use
- No RCT data in children; EMA monograph restricted to adults ≥18 years
- Insufficient evidence to recommend for pediatric populations

### Lactation
- No human data; EMA HMPC 2016: safety during breastfeeding not established; use not recommended
- Precautionary avoidance advised

---

## Product Quality and Consumer Information Concerns

**Bargsten & Seifert 2025** (88 products analyzed, PMID 40042557):
- Only **6% of food supplements** reported ADR information
- **100% of dietary supplements** reported no ADR information
- 100% of herbal medicinal products (pharmaceutical-grade) reported ADR data and stated no known interactions
- **Consumer information reliability:** Food and dietary supplements show "questionable reliability" and "significant gaps in consumer information"

**Implication:** Patients using non-pharmaceutical-grade devil's claw products (food supplements, dietary supplements) receive inadequate safety information.

## Safety Summary for Practice

| Population | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Adults with LBP or OA | Generally safe at 50–100 mg harpagoside/day for up to 54 weeks; monitor for GI effects |
| Gastric/duodenal ulcer | Contraindicated |
| Pregnancy | Contraindicated (oxytocic risk) |
| Breastfeeding | Avoid (insufficient data) |
| Hypertension | Use with caution; monitor BP (one case report of BP elevation at 500 mg/day) |
| Gallstones | Use with caution; potential to trigger biliary colic |
| Warfarin users | Monitor INR (theoretical interaction) |
| Antidiabetic drug users | Monitor blood glucose |
| Children | Insufficient data; not recommended |
| Hypersensitivity to Harpagophytum | Contraindicated |
