# Devil's Claw - Traditional Uses

**Species:** *Harpagophytum procumbens* DC. (primary); *H. zeyheri* Decne. (secondary)
**Common names:** Devil's claw, Grapple plant, Wood spider; Teufelskralle (German), Griffe du diable (French)
**Origin:** Southern Africa (Kalahari Desert region; extends into Namibia, Botswana, South Africa)
**Traditional part used:** Dried secondary root tubers

---

## African Traditional Medicine (Primary Tradition)

Devil's claw is an **exclusively African botanical** — it has no history in Ayurveda, TCM, or other Asian traditions. Its traditional range is limited to the Kalahari Desert region, particularly Namibia, Botswana, and the Northern Cape of South Africa.

### San and Khoikhoi Peoples (Southern Africa)
- **Pain relief:** Root decoctions used for various types of pain, particularly joint pain and rheumatic conditions
- **Fever reduction:** Preparation of root taken internally for febrile states
- **Digestive support:** Used for stomach complaints and to stimulate appetite
- **Childbirth:** Topical application of root preparations reportedly used during labor; also possibly taken internally — this is the likely source of the oxytocic concern documented in regulatory contraindications

### Documented Traditional Medicinal Uses (Brendler 2021, Alt Med Rev 2008)
1. **Musculoskeletal pain** — joint pain, arthritis-type symptoms, back pain
2. **Digestive disorders** — indigestion, appetite stimulation, liver and gallbladder complaints
3. **Fever** — febrifuge
4. **Diabetes** — blood sugar management (traditional claim; no confirmed clinical evidence)
5. **Skin conditions** — topical application of root preparations for boils, sores, and wounds (limited documentation)

### First Documented Medicinal Account
- **Brendler 2021** (PMID 34451822) notes the first explicit medicinal account was **recorded 1901**, making devil's claw a relatively recently documented botanical despite older folk use.
- European awareness of the plant grew through colonial botanical expeditions in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

---

## European Phytotherapy (20th–21st Century)

Devil's claw entered **European phytomedicine in the 1960s–1970s** as African plant medicines gained broader attention. It was not part of traditional European herbal medicine (unlike valerian, chamomile, or St. John's wort).

### German Commission E
- Approved devil's claw root for three indications:
  1. **Dyspepsia** (digestive disturbances)
  2. **Appetite stimulation** (loss of appetite / anorexia)
  3. **Degenerative musculoskeletal disorders** (Erkrankungen des Bewegungsapparates)
- These approvals represent a formalization of traditional use into the German regulatory framework for phytomedicines.

### European Medicines Agency (EMA HMPC 2016)
- Classified as a **traditional herbal medicinal product** (≥30 years of use including ≥15 years in the EU; plausible efficacy but insufficient clinical trial evidence for "well-established use" status)
- Approved traditional use indications:
  1. Relief of minor joint pain (musculoskeletal)
  2. Relief of mild digestive disorders (bloating, flatulence)
  3. Temporary loss of appetite
- European Pharmacopoeia has listed both *H. procumbens* and *H. zeyheri* since 2003

## Traditional Preparation Methods

### Decoction (Traditional African Method)
- Secondary root tubers harvested, sliced, and sun-dried
- Boiled in water for 15–30 minutes to produce a bitter tea/decoction
- Consumed as a tea for joint pain, fever, and digestive complaints

### Tinctures (European Phytotherapy)
- Typical ratios: 1:5 to 1:10 drug-to-extract ratios
- Menstruum: water to 90% ethanol (variable)
- Note: ethanolic extracts generally yield lower harpagoside content than aqueous preparations; this affects therapeutic dosing

### Dried Root Powder / Capsules (Modern)
- Cryoground powder preserves iridoid content better than heat-processed forms
- Harpadol (Chantre 2000 trials) used cryoground powder at 6 × 435 mg capsules/day

## Traditions Not Represented

- **Ayurveda:** No use — not a native plant; no Ayurvedic record
- **Traditional Chinese Medicine:** No use — not found in the Chinese Materia Medica
- **Native American traditions:** No use — plant is native only to southern Africa
- **Medieval European herbalism:** No use — unknown to Europe before colonial contact

## Traditional Use Notes for Practitioners

1. **The bitter taste** is therapeutically significant in traditional African and European phytotherapy contexts — digestive bitters stimulate gastric acid and bile secretion, consistent with the appetite-stimulation and dyspepsia indications.

2. **Distinction from other "claw" plants:** Devil's claw (*Harpagophytum* spp.) is entirely unrelated to cat's claw (*Uncaria tomentosa*), despite superficially similar folk use for inflammation.

3. **Harvesting sustainability:** Wild-harvested from the Kalahari; commercial demand has raised concerns about overharvesting in Namibia and Botswana. Several certification programs now exist for sustainably sourced material.

4. **Species equivalence:** *H. procumbens* is the pharmacopoeial standard, but *H. zeyheri* is accepted in the European Pharmacopoeia as an equivalent source. Commercial preparations may contain either species or both; harpagoside content can vary (0–4% in *H. procumbens*; 0–3% in *H. zeyheri*).
