# Pau d'Arco (Handroanthus impetiginosus) - Traditional Uses

## Overview

Pau d'arco has an extensive traditional use history centered in South America, particularly Brazil, Bolivia, and Argentina. It has been used for at least centuries by indigenous peoples across these regions. The herb entered global commerce from the 1960s onward and is now sold internationally as "pau d'arco," "lapacho," and "taheebo."

**Note**: No WHO monograph, Commission E monograph, EMA/HMPC assessment, ESCOP monograph, or AHP monograph exists for pau d'arco, reflecting the absence of formal regulatory validation of traditional claims.

## Primary Traditions and Geographic Origins

### Indigenous South American (Core Tradition)
- **Peoples**: Guaraní, Tupinambá, and other indigenous groups of Brazil, Bolivia, Paraguay, and Argentina
- **Documented use period**: Pre-Columbian to present; systematic ethnopharmacological documentation from 20th century
- **Primary medicinal part**: Inner bark of trunk or branches (comprising approximately 88% of traditional preparations across documented sources; Bezerra et al. 2022)
- **Preparation tradition**: Decoction (bark boiled in water) is the primary traditional preparation

### South American Folk Medicine (Lapacho tradition)
- **Region**: Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Paraguay
- **Local name**: "Lapacho" (Argentina/Bolivia/Paraguay); "Pau d'arco" (Brazil); "Ipe roxo" (Brazil, purple-flowered species)
- **Commercial expansion**: Late 20th century global commerce as "taheebo" (Japanese market terminology introduced 1960s–70s)

## Traditional Indications

### Infections
- Bacterial infections (including syphilis — historically significant indication)
- Fungal infections, Candida overgrowth
- Malaria and malarial fevers
- Trypanosomiasis (American sleeping sickness / Chagas disease)
- General febrile illness

### Gastrointestinal
- Stomach disorders, gastric complaints
- Inflammatory bowel conditions (traditional, not validated)

### Genitourinary
- Bladder disorders
- Urinary tract infections

### Pain and Inflammation
- General pain (anti-inflammatory use)
- Dysmenorrhea (menstrual cramps) — the only indication with any clinical trial data (McClure 2022)
- Arthritis and joint pain

### Cancer
- Multiple tumor types — traditional anticancer reputation drove NCI interest in lapachol from the 1960s
- Note: No evidence base supports this use for the whole-herb preparation

### Skin Conditions
- Wound healing, skin infections (topical application of decoction)

## Traditional Preparations

### Decoction (Primary Traditional Form)
- Inner bark (2–4 g dried bark per cup) simmered in water for 15–20 minutes
- One to three cups daily
- Aqueous extraction yields limited naphthoquinone content (lapachol is poorly water-soluble); traditional efficacy may be mediated by polar constituents (flavonoids, phenolic glycosides) rather than naphthoquinones

### Bark as Raw Material
- Inner bark of trunk or branches (not outer bark)
- Bark constitutes 88% of traditional preparations across Brazilian, Bolivian, and Argentinian use patterns (Bezerra 2022)

## Relationship to Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese Medicine

Pau d'arco has **no documented place in Ayurveda or Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)**. It is exclusively a New World botanical with South American indigenous and folk medicine roots. Global popularity is entirely a product of 20th-century commercial expansion, not traditional cross-cultural adoption.

## Species Identity and Nomenclature Issues

Significant confusion exists in both traditional use and commerce:
- *Handroanthus impetiginosus* (Mart. ex DC.) Mattos = current accepted scientific name
- *Tabebuia impetiginosa* (Mart. ex DC.) Standl. = major synonym still widely used in literature
- *Tabebuia avellanedae* Lorentz ex Griseb. = synonym used in commerce and in the only clinical trial (McClure 2022)
- Commercial products are frequently mislabeled or use these names interchangeably (Gómez Castellanos 2009, PMID 18992801)
- Multiple *Handroanthus* species (and possibly related genera) may be sold under "pau d'arco" labeling

## Ethnopharmacological Assessment (Gómez Castellanos 2009)

Comprehensive analysis (PMID 18992801) concluded:
- T. impetiginosa occupies a genuine global ethnopharmacological niche
- Commercial product quality and species identity are significant concerns
- Bioscientific evidence insufficient to substantiate most therapeutic claims
- Further research on chemically characterized extracts is required
