# Oil of Oregano - Traditional Uses

**Important species note:** Traditional medicine systems used aerial parts (herb), infusions, and local preparations from wild *Origanum* species. The modern "oil of oregano" supplement (steam-distilled essential oil or emulsified preparations) is a 20th-century commercial development distinct from traditional whole-herb use. Traditional use attributions below apply primarily to the herb; essential oil as a therapeutic concentrate has no direct historical precedent in most traditions.

---

## Mediterranean / European Traditional Medicine

### Greek and Mediterranean Folk Medicine (Ancient–18th century)
- **Tradition:** Ancient Greek, Byzantine, and folk Mediterranean medicine
- **Preparations:** Fresh or dried herb as infusion, decoction, poultice, and steam inhalation
- **Uses documented:**
  - Respiratory: colds, fever, cough, bronchitis, catarrh (inhaled steam from herb or decoction)
  - Digestive: indigestion, bloating, flatulence, intestinal spasms, stomach cramps
  - Antimicrobial/antiseptic: wound treatment, skin infections, oral hygiene
  - Parasitic infections: gastrointestinal worm and parasite expulsion
  - Urinary tract: mild UTI and urinary discomfort
- **Species:** *Origanum vulgare*, *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (Greek oregano), *O. dictamnus* (Dittany of Crete)
- **Sources:** Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (1st century CE); traditional Cretan folk medicine records

### European Phytotherapy (19th–20th century)
- **Tradition:** Western European herbal medicine
- **Preparations:** Herbal tea (infusion), tincture, topical poultice
- **Documented uses:** Cough, bronchitis, respiratory congestion, digestive aid, antispasmodic for GI complaints
- **Regulatory precedent:** EMA/HMPC recognizes traditional use for related species:
  - *O. majorana* (sweet marjoram): Traditional use for mild indigestion (bloating/flatulence) and irritated skin around nostrils
  - *O. dictamnus* (Dittany of Crete): Traditional use for cough associated with cold, mild GI spasms and flatulence
- **Note:** No EMA/HMPC monograph exists for *O. vulgare* itself. Related species monographs establish Origanum genus precedent for GI and respiratory traditional use.

## North American Traditional Use

### Health Canada Recognition (Contemporary)
- **Status:** Health Canada Natural Health Products Directorate accepts oregano as a natural health product
- **Recognized traditional uses:** Antioxidant; digestive aid; antimicrobial
- **Basis:** Traditional use recognition under the Natural Health Products Regulations
- **Note:** This is a contemporary regulatory recognition of traditional use, not a historical ethnobotanical record

### North American Eclectic Medicine (19th–early 20th century)
- **Tradition:** Eclectic physicians (North America)
- **Uses:** Oregano herb preparations used for digestive upset, respiratory infections, and as a stimulating expectorant
- **Preparations:** Fluid extract, infusion

## Indigenous and Traditional Cultures (Broader Genus)

### Middle Eastern and Levantine Medicine
- **Tradition:** Unani and Arab traditional medicine (9th–16th century)
- **Uses:** Origanum species (za'atar type) used for respiratory complaints, digestive disorders, and as an antimicrobial/antiseptic
- **Preparations:** Decoctions, steam inhalation, poultices

### Mexican and Latin American Folk Use
- **Tradition:** Mexican folk medicine (*O. vulgare* and related species used as "orégano")
- **Uses:** Digestive remedy, cough suppressant, menstrual regulator
- **Note:** Abortifacient use recorded — consistent with historical contraindication in pregnancy (see Safety)

- Bacterial and fungal infections
- Parasitic infections

## Traditional Preparations

### Herbal Infusion (Tea)
- **Preparation:** 1–2 teaspoons dried herb per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10–15 minutes covered; strain
- **Traditional dose:** 1–3 cups daily
- **Indicated for (traditionally):** Respiratory symptoms, digestive upset, general antimicrobial support
- **Note:** Aqueous infusion extracts primarily hydrophilic phenolics; essential oil constituents (carvacrol, thymol) are largely volatile and minimally extracted in water at standard brewing temperatures

### Decoction
- **Preparation:** Herb simmered 10–20 minutes in water; more concentrated than infusion
- **Traditional use:** Stronger preparations for respiratory or GI infections

### Steam Inhalation
- **Preparation:** Fresh or dried herb added to a bowl of hot water; towel draped over head; inhale steam for 5–10 minutes
- **Traditional use:** Cough, bronchitis, sinus congestion
- **Note:** Delivers volatile compounds (carvacrol, thymol) directly to respiratory mucosa; distinct mechanism from oral ingestion

### Poultice
- **Preparation:** Crushed fresh herb applied directly to skin or wrapped in cloth
- **Traditional use:** Wound antisepsis, skin infections, headache (applied to forehead)

### Macerated Oil (Culinary/Traditional)
- **Preparation:** Fresh herb packed in olive oil, infused for several weeks
- **Traditional use:** Topical antiseptic, wound care, skin conditions
- **Distinction:** Macerated oil ≠ steam-distilled essential oil; far lower carvacrol concentration

## Key Distinction: Traditional Herb vs Modern Essential Oil Products

| Aspect | Traditional herb use | Modern oil of oregano supplement |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Dried herb, infusion, tincture | Steam-distilled essential oil (emulsified) |
| Carvacrol concentration | Low to moderate (variable) | Very high (typically 50–86% of oil) |
| Historical precedent | Centuries of documented use | Primarily post-1990s commercial development |
| Evidence basis | Folk tradition, some pharmacopoeial recognition | Small number of uncontrolled clinical studies |
| Regulatory status | Traditional use recognition (EMA, Health Canada) | GRAS food flavoring (FDA); NHP (Health Canada) |

The essential oil is a concentrate that did not exist in traditional medicine. Modern emulsified oil products derive their reputation partly from the traditional use record of the whole herb, and partly from antimicrobial in vitro data. The two should not be conflated.

---

## Evidence Quality Note

Traditional use claims for oil of oregano specifically lack direct historical documentation. The traditional evidence base belongs to the herb; the essential oil inherits these claims by extrapolation. No WHO monograph or Commission E monograph exists for *O. vulgare* essential oil — the regulatory bodies have not formally codified traditional use for this preparation.
