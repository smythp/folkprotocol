# Oil of Oregano - Active Compounds

---

## Primary Bioactive Constituents

### Carvacrol
- **Chemical class:** Monoterpenoid phenol (5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol)
- **Role:** Principal therapeutic marker compound; dominant antimicrobial and antioxidant constituent
- **Typical range in essential oil:** 36–86% depending on subspecies, growing conditions, and harvest timing
- **High-carvacrol subspecies:** *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (Greek/Mediterranean oregano): typically 60–86%
- **Common oregano (subsp. *vulgare*):** Often <20% carvacrol; not suitable for therapeutic use without carvacrol standardization
- **European Pharmacopoeia requirement:** Combined carvacrol + thymol ≥60% of essential oil (Ph. Eur. 01/2008:1880)
- **Mechanisms (preclinical):**
  - Disrupts bacterial cell membranes (primary antimicrobial mechanism)
  - Inhibits NF-κB signaling (anti-inflammatory)
  - COX-2 inhibition (anti-inflammatory)
  - Inhibits α-amylase and α-glucosidase (antidiabetic; in vitro only)
  - DPPH radical scavenging (antioxidant; in vitro)
- **Antimicrobial spectrum (in vitro):** Active against *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *B. cereus*, MRSA, *H. pylori*, *Blastocystis hominis*, *Entamoeba* species, *Candida* species
- **Preclinical anticancer activity:** Breast, liver, and lung carcinoma models; no human data

### Thymol
- **Chemical class:** Monoterpenoid phenol (2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol); isomer of carvacrol
- **Role:** Secondary marker compound; synergistic with carvacrol
- **Typical range:** 1–27% in high-carvacrol subspecies (inversely correlated with carvacrol in most chemotypes)
- **Mechanisms:** Similar to carvacrol — membrane disruption, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory
- **Metabolism:** Primarily by CYP2B6 in humans (vs CYP2A6 for carvacrol)

## Secondary Constituents

| Compound | Class | Typical % | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| p-cymene | Monoterpene hydrocarbon | 3–25% | Precursor in carvacrol biosynthesis; antimicrobial synergist |
| γ-terpinene | Monoterpene | 5–20% | Antioxidant synergist; antimicrobial |
| Linalool | Monoterpene alcohol | 1–5% | Mild sedative/anxiolytic in aromatherapy; minor in oregano |
| β-myrcene | Monoterpene | 1–3% | Anti-inflammatory preclinical activity |
| Rosmarinic acid | Phenylpropanoid | Trace–moderate | Major antioxidant in aqueous oregano extract; not essential oil |
| Luteolin | Flavonoid | Trace | Anti-inflammatory; predominantly in herb, not distilled oil |
| Apigenin | Flavonoid | Trace | Mild anxiolytic; predominantly in herb, not distilled oil |

**Note on rosmarinic acid:** This compound is a primary antioxidant in oregano herb and aqueous extracts (studied by Nurmi et al. 2006) but is NOT present in steam-distilled essential oil. Products based on phenolic herb extracts have a different compound profile from essential oil products.

## Chemotype Variation

Oregano essential oil composition varies dramatically based on:
1. **Subspecies/botanical identity** — subsp. *hirtum* has highest carvacrol; subsp. *vulgare* often has low carvacrol, high sabinene/β-ocimene chemotype
2. **Geographic origin** — Greek, Turkish, and Spanish oregano oils differ substantially
3. **Harvest timing** — carvacrol highest at full bloom
4. **Altitude and growing conditions** — higher altitude generally increases monoterpene content
5. **Distillation parameters** — longer distillation extracts heavier fractions

**Practical implication:** Products labeled "oil of oregano" with unspecified subspecies or carvacrol percentage may provide very little carvacrol. This is the primary quality issue in the supplement market.

## Standardization

### Official Standards
- **European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur. 01/2008:1880):**
  - Minimum 25 mL essential oil per kg dry herb
  - Minimum 60% combined carvacrol + thymol in the essential oil
  - Quantification by GC-FID (ISO 13171 method)
- **ISO 13171:** Standard method for GC analysis of oregano essential oil composition

### Commercial Product Standards
- **Common commercial range:** 50–80% carvacrol
- **High-potency products:** Advertise 80–86% carvacrol
- **Standardized extracts (phenolic):** Standardized to total rosmarinic acid or total phenolics (different from essential oil products)
- **Preferred species for therapeutic use:** *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (explicitly named); should appear on product labeling

### What to Look for on Labels
1. Species name: *Origanum vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (or "Greek oregano" or "Mediterranean oregano")
2. Carvacrol percentage: ≥60% minimum; 70–80% typical for therapeutic preparations
3. Dilution/carrier: Emulsified oil in softgels or liquid in carrier oil (olive oil common)

## Bioavailability

### Absorption
- **Route:** Oral ingestion; rapid GI absorption due to high lipophilicity of carvacrol and thymol
- **Absorption confirmation (human):** Nurmi et al. 2006 — urinary phenolic acid excretion markedly increased at 600 mg/day oregano phenolic extract, confirming systemic absorption
- **Limitation:** No human plasma pharmacokinetic data (Cmax, Tmax, AUC, half-life) for commercial oil of oregano preparations identified in the literature

### Factors Reducing Oral Bioavailability
- **High volatility:** Carvacrol evaporates readily; capsule fill integrity affects dose delivery
- **Low water solubility:** Reduces dissolution and absorption from aqueous GI environment
- **Sensitivity to heat and acidic pH:** May partially degrade in stomach acid
- **First-pass metabolism:** Significant hepatic extraction likely, though not characterized in humans

### Emulsification Enhancement
- **Rationale:** Emulsification improves aqueous dispersibility, particle size reduction, and likely oral bioavailability
- **Commercial standard:** Most therapeutic products use emulsified oil (softgels) or oil in carrier lipid
- **Evidence:** Force et al. 2000 used emulsified preparations; presumed superior to neat oil but not confirmed in direct human PK comparison

### Nanoencapsulation (Emerging)
- Currently under investigation to further improve delivery and stability
- No human clinical data yet available for nanoencapsulated formulations

### Metabolism
- **Carvacrol:** Primary metabolizing enzyme is CYP2A6 in humans; CYP1A2 and CYP2B6 contribute secondarily (Sharifi-Rad et al. 2018)
- **Thymol:** Primarily metabolized by CYP2B6
- **Excretion:** As glucuronide and sulfate conjugates via urine
- **CYP interaction implications:** Chronic high-dose use could theoretically affect metabolism of other CYP2A6 substrates (see Safety file)

## Key Gaps in Compound Knowledge

- No human plasma pharmacokinetic studies for carvacrol or thymol after oral supplementation
- No dose-response data in humans for carvacrol levels achieved
- Relative bioavailability of emulsified vs non-emulsified preparations not characterized in humans
- Synergistic effects between carvacrol, thymol, and minor constituents not validated clinically
- Nanoencapsulation efficacy unproven in human trials
