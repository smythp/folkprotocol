# Oil of Oregano - Safety

---

## Hepatotoxicity Assessment

**LiverTox Likelihood Score: E — Unlikely cause of clinically apparent liver injury**

- No published reports of serum enzyme elevations attributable to oil of oregano in clinical use
- Not implicated in any major international DILI registry (US DILI Network, Spanish DILI Registry, Latin American DILI Network)
- Nurmi et al. 2006 RCT (n=45, 300–600 mg/day oregano phenolic extract, 4 weeks): no changes in liver enzyme levels
- FDA designation: Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) as food flavoring

*Source: LiverTox NBK591556 (2023)*

---

## Toxicology — Animal Data

**EFSA 90-day rat oral toxicity (O. vulgare subsp. hirtum essential oil):**
- **NOAEL:** 200 mg/kg body weight per day (highest dose tested; no adverse effects observed)
- **Margin of exposure (humans at typical dietary exposure):** ~4,000 — indicating substantial safety margin
- **Mutagenicity:** Not mutagenic in standard battery of tests
- **Human equivalent dose extrapolation (body weight scaling 60 kg human):** ~12,000 mg/day NOAEL equivalent — far above typical supplement doses (150–600 mg/day)

*Source: EFSA FEEDAP Panel 2019 (PMC7008907)*

## Known Adverse Effects

### Gastrointestinal
- Heartburn, acid reflux (most commonly reported)
- Nausea, vomiting
- Diarrhea or constipation
- GI discomfort at higher doses
- *Onset:* Typically with higher doses or in individuals with GI sensitivity
- *Management:* Take with food; reduce dose or discontinue

### Neurological (Higher Doses)
- Dizziness
- Headache
- *Basis:* Case reports and traditional use observations; not systematically characterized

### Dermatological / Allergic
- Contact dermatitis (topical application without adequate dilution)
- Allergic rhinitis
- *Population at risk:* Individuals with Lamiaceae (mint family) sensitivity or known allergy to basil, mint, lavender, marjoram, thyme, sage
- Skin/eye irritation from undiluted essential oil (EFSA risk classification)

## Contraindications

### Pregnancy — Contraindicated
- **Rating:** Contraindicated at medicinal doses
- **Basis:** Abortifacient properties documented in traditional medicine; historically used in folk medicine as an emmenagogue and abortifacient
- **Evidence level:** Traditional use evidence; no formal human teratogenicity studies (none expected given the contraindication)
- **Culinary use:** Small amounts as food spice not addressed by available safety data; medicinal supplement doses should be avoided
- **Regulatory note:** Abortifacient property noted in LiverTox (NBK591556)

### Lactation
- **Rating:** Avoid medicinal doses; insufficient data
- **Basis:** No human lactation pharmacokinetic data; carvacrol is lipophilic and may transfer into breast milk; infant exposure not characterized
- **Culinary use:** Not addressed; medicinal doses not recommended until data available
- **Source:** Drugs and Lactation Database (LactMed, PMID 30000904) — oregano entry indicates insufficient data

### Lamiaceae Family Allergy
- Potential cross-reactivity with mint, basil, sage, thyme, rosemary, lavender, marjoram
- Screen for family history of sensitivity before recommending

---

## Drug Interactions

### Anticoagulants / Antiplatelet Drugs
- **Interaction:** Theoretical potentiation of anticoagulation due to antithrombotic properties of carvacrol
- **Evidence level:** Theoretical; not confirmed in human studies
- **Drugs at risk:** Warfarin, heparin, aspirin, clopidogrel, novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs)
- **Recommendation:** Monitor INR in patients on warfarin; exercise caution and disclose use to prescriber

### CYP2A6 Substrates
- **Mechanism:** Carvacrol is metabolized by CYP2A6 (and secondarily CYP1A2, CYP2B6); prolonged high-dose use could affect CYP2A6 activity and alter metabolism of other CYP2A6 substrates
- **CYP2A6 substrates of clinical relevance:** Coumarin, nicotine, letrozole, fadrozole, SM-12502, efavirenz
- **Evidence level:** In vitro and animal data only (Bajagai et al. 2021 — broiler model); human drug interaction studies absent
- **Clinical relevance:** Likely low at typical supplement doses; theoretically relevant at chronic high doses

### Steroid Hormones (Animal Signal)
- **Mechanism:** Bajagai et al. 2021 (broiler chickens, 2% dietary supplementation) showed altered steroid hormone gene expression patterns
- **Evidence level:** Animal study only; human relevance uncertain
- **Relevant drugs:** Potential concern with hormone-sensitive conditions or steroidal medications; speculative extrapolation from animal model
- **Note:** 2% dietary supplementation in chickens far exceeds typical human supplement doses; signal requires human investigation before clinical guidance

### Diabetes Medications
- **Mechanism:** In vitro inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase (antidiabetic activity in cell models)
- **Evidence level:** In vitro only; no human blood glucose data
- **Theoretical risk:** May additively lower blood glucose in patients on insulin or oral hypoglycemics
- **Recommendation:** Monitor blood glucose; disclose use to prescriber

---

## Specific Populations

### Children
- No pediatric dosing data; no safety studies in children identified
- **Recommendation:** Not established for use in children; avoid until data available

### Elderly
- No specific safety data
- Standard caution regarding drug interactions applies; polypharmacy common in elderly

### Immunocompromised
- No specific safety data; in vitro antimicrobial activity does not predict benefit or harm in immunocompromised hosts
- Any infectious indication should be managed with evidence-based treatments

---

## Long-Term Safety

- Longest human safety study identified: 4 weeks (Nurmi et al. 2006; n=45; no adverse effects)
- No human long-term safety data (>4 weeks) identified
- EFSA animal NOAEL (200 mg/kg/day × 90 days) provides some reassurance for subchronic exposure
- Bajagai et al. 2021 animal signal for hormonal/drug interference with chronic high-dose exposure warrants human investigation
