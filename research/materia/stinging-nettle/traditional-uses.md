# Stinging Nettle (Urtica dioica) - Traditional Uses

## Overview

Urtica dioica has one of the longest and broadest ethnobotanical records among European medicinal plants, with uses spanning food, fiber, and medicine across multiple centuries and traditions. The leaf/herb and root carry distinct traditional therapeutic profiles.

---

## European Herbal Tradition

### Medieval and Early Modern Europe (10th–18th Century)

**Urinary and Diuretic Uses (Leaf)**
- Documented in European herbals as a diuretic and "purifying" herb for the urinary tract
- Used for kidney gravel, urinary retention, and inflammatory urinary complaints
- Nicholas Culpeper (17th century): recommended nettle for "expelling gravel and stone from the reins and bladder" and for relieving stings, bites, and sores
- John Gerard's *Herball* (1597): described nettle leaf for "stopping of the belly" and urinary gravel; noted the sting as caustic but medicinally useful
- Used as spring tonic and blood purifier, consistent with traditional European seasonal detox concepts

**Respiratory and Allergic Uses**
- European folk medicine: fresh or dried leaf preparations for hayfever and respiratory catarrh (historically called "spring catarrh")
- Freeze-dried preparations were later formalized in naturopathic practice for allergic rhinitis

**Rheumatic and Joint Uses (Leaf/Sting)**
- Urtication (intentional stinging of painful joints with fresh nettle plant) practiced since Greco-Roman antiquity
- Documented by Pliny the Elder (1st century AD): nettle sting applied to arthritic joints for relief
- Persisted in European folk medicine through the 20th century, particularly in rural Britain and Germany
- German Commission E (1990) formally recognized this tradition, approving nettle leaf for "rheumatic complaints (supportive)"

**Nutritional-Medicinal Uses**
- Widely consumed as a spring food (boiled or steamed to remove sting); high in iron, calcium, vitamins A and K
- Used for anemia, fatigue, and nutritional deficiency — supports traditional hematopoietic reputation
- Traditional "nettle soup" and nettle beer documented across Northern Europe

### 19th–20th Century European Eclectic/Naturopathic Tradition
- Eclectic physicians (American): Used nettle for hematuria (blood in urine), menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding), and as a hemostatic
- German naturopathic tradition: Nettle root adopted for prostate complaints in early 20th century, leading to the formal BPH indication later codified by Commission E (1987)

## Ayurvedic Tradition

**Sanskrit name**: Bichhu buti (colloquial); Vrischikali (some regional names)
**System**: Ayurveda (Indian traditional medicine)

- Classified in Ayurvedic texts under herbs with "ruksha" (dry) and "ushna" (hot) properties
- Traditional uses: diuretic, for skin diseases, hemorrhagic conditions (leaf), and as a tonic
- Applied externally for rheumatic pain and skin eruptions
- Used for urinary stones and dysuria (painful urination)
- Less centrally featured in classical Ayurveda than in European traditions; more prominent in folk/regional Indian practice
- Modern Ayurvedic practitioners: used for BPH, arthritis, and metabolic complaints, largely informed by European clinical evidence

---

## Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)

**Chinese name**: Xun ma (荨麻)
- Urtica dioica and Urtica thunbergiana used in Chinese herbal medicine
- Traditional uses: expel wind-dampness, relieve pain, reduce swelling
- Applied for rheumatic pain, muscle aches, and as a diuretic
- Less prominent in classical TCM than in European traditions; used more as a folk/regional remedy
- Modern TCM practitioners sometimes incorporate nettle for inflammatory conditions and urinary complaints

---

## North American Indigenous / Folk Uses

- Native American traditions: Several tribes used Urtica dioica (or North American Urtica species) for joint pain and as a food source
- Early American folk medicine adopted European nettle traditions; used for diuresis, kidney gravel, and spring tonics
- Homeopathic tradition (Urtica urens): Used for burns, gout, urticaria, and lactation insufficiency — distinct from phytotherapeutic nettle use

## Regulatory Codification of Traditional Use

### German Commission E (1990 — Leaf/Herb)
Formal recognition of traditional indications:
- Irrigation therapy for inflammatory disease of the lower urinary tract
- Prevention and treatment of kidney gravel (supportive irrigation)
- Rheumatic complaints (supportive)
- Dosing: 8–12 g dried herb daily; adequate fluid intake required

### German Commission E (1987 — Root)
- Symptomatic treatment of micturition disorders in BPH stages I and II
- Represents the formal phytotherapeutic codification of the traditional European prostate use

### EMA/HMPC (2011 — Leaf)
- Traditional use category (based on ≥30 years of use, including ≥15 years in EU):
  - Adjuvant to increased fluid intake in minor urinary complaints
  - Temporary relief of symptoms of mild arthritis
- Reference: EMA/HMPC/508015/2007; European Pharmacopoeia monograph 01/2008:1897

### Health Canada (NHP Monograph)
- Recognized uses: Diuretic (leaf), relief of mild rheumatic conditions (leaf), relief of LUTS associated with BPH (root)

## Traditional Preparations

| Preparation | Part | Traditional Use |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh leaf (urtication) | Leaf | Joint pain, rheumatism |
| Dried leaf infusion (tea) | Leaf | Diuresis, urinary tract, spring tonic |
| Boiled/cooked leaf | Leaf | Nutritional (iron, vitamins), anemia |
| Tincture (leaf) | Leaf | Urinary complaints, allergic conditions |
| Root decoction | Root | Prostate/urinary symptoms (BPH) |
| Root extract | Root | BPH (modern phytotherapy) |
| Topical juice/poultice | Leaf | Skin conditions, eczema, wounds |

## Key Notes on Traditional Use vs Modern Evidence

- BPH indication (root): Traditional use predates clinical evidence; European naturopathic recognition preceded modern RCTs by decades
- Rheumatic/joint use (leaf, urtication): Traditional use from antiquity; supported by exploratory clinical evidence but not yet confirmed by rigorous RCTs
- Diuretic/urinary irrigation use (leaf): Traditional use aligns with aquaretic (water-promoting) rather than true natriuretic pharmacology; modern trials focus on other indications
- Allergic rhinitis: Traditional use as antihistamine herb emerged in 20th century; informed by freeze-dried leaf preparations in naturopathic practice
