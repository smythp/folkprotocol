# White Willow Bark — Traditional Uses

*Salix alba / Salicis cortex*

## Overview

White willow bark has one of the longest documented histories of any medicinal plant, with use spanning at least 3,500 years across multiple traditions. Its salicylate content underpins the historical use as an antipyretic and analgesic — the same pathway that led to the development of aspirin in 1899.

---

## European Tradition

**Oldest documented use; most extensively recorded.**

### Ancient Mediterranean
- **Egypt (~1550 BCE):** Ebers Papyrus records willow bark for pain and inflammation
- **Ancient Greece:** Hippocrates (~400 BCE) documented chewing willow bark for fever, pain, and inflammation — one of the earliest written records of medicinal plant use
- **Rome:** Dioscorides (1st century CE) and Pliny the Elder described willow bark for fever, gout, and articular complaints

### Medieval and Early Modern Europe
- Willow bark decoctions used for fever, rheumatics, and headache throughout medieval European medicine
- Common in herbalist traditions across Germany, England, and France
- Reverend Edmund Stone's 1763 letter to the Royal Society of London formalized clinical observation that willow bark (Salix alba) effectively reduced fever — directly precipitating the eventual isolation of salicin (Leroux, 1829) and subsequent development of aspirin

### Regulatory Recognition of Traditional Use
- German Commission E approved willow bark for febrile illness, rheumatic complaints, and headaches — reflecting longstanding German herbal medicine tradition (Volksmedizin)
- EMA HMPC (2017) granted "traditional use" status for willow bark preparations based on ≥30 years of documented medicinal use in the EU
- ESCOP (2017) monograph codifies the European phytotherapy tradition

---

## Indigenous North American Traditions

Several Salix species (not necessarily S. alba but related willows) were used by multiple Indigenous peoples:

- **Bark decoctions:** Used for fever, pain, inflammation
- **Applications documented:** Headache, toothache, rheumatism, muscle pain
- **Species:** Primarily native Salix species (S. nigra, S. lucida, and others) rather than the European S. alba, though pharmacological properties are closely parallel

---

## Traditional Preparations

### Decoction (most traditional preparation)
- Bark boiled in water; most common historical form
- **Dose:** WHO monograph standardizes this at 6–12 g powdered bark per day in two divided doses
- Traditional decoctions typically prepared by simmering 1–2 teaspoons of bark per cup of water for 10–15 minutes

### Tea/Infusion
- Less efficient than decoction for bark material; historically used but extracts less active constituents than boiling
- Generally considered inferior to decoction for therapeutic effect

### Tincture
- Tincture preparations appeared in European pharmacy from the 16th–17th centuries
- Standardized fluid extracts became common in 19th century pharmacy

### Chewing Bark
- Earliest recorded use (Hippocrates); direct mastication of fresh or dried bark
- Not a common modern preparation

## Historical Significance

The history of white willow is directly tied to the origin of modern anti-inflammatory pharmacology:

1. **~1550 BCE:** Egyptian use documented (Ebers Papyrus)
2. **~400 BCE:** Hippocrates documents fever and pain relief
3. **1763:** Rev. Edmund Stone formalizes clinical documentation in England
4. **1829:** Salicin isolated by Henri Leroux (France)
5. **1838:** Salicylic acid synthesized from salicin by Raffaele Piria (Italy)
6. **1853:** Aspirin precursor (acetylsalicylic acid) first synthesized by Charles Frédéric Gerhardt
7. **1897:** Bayer chemist Felix Hoffmann synthesizes stable acetylsalicylic acid; aspirin commercialized 1899
8. **1960s–present:** Resurgence of standardized willow bark extract research as NSAID alternative

The botanical preceded and directly inspired the pharmaceutical — making white willow one of the clearest examples of ethnobotanical knowledge leading to modern drug development.
