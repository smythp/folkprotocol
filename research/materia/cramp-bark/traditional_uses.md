# Cramp Bark - Traditional Uses

## Overview

Viburnum opulus (cramp bark) has documented use across multiple ethnobotanical traditions, primarily as an antispasmodic and smooth muscle relaxant. The bark (cortex) is the traditional medicinal part in Western and Eastern European traditions; the fruit is used separately in Turkish folk medicine.

---

## North American Traditions

### Indigenous and Eclectic Physicians
- **Indication**: Uterine spasms, dysmenorrhea, threatened miscarriage, afterpains
- **Tradition**: North American indigenous use; adopted extensively by 19th-century Eclectic physicians
- **Historical context**: Eclectics (particularly Felter, Lloyd) documented cramp bark as a leading antispasmodic for conditions characterized by smooth muscle spasm
- **Preparation**: Bark decoction or fluid extract
- **Part used**: Dried stem bark (cortex)

---

## European Traditions

### British Herbal Medicine
- **Source**: British Herbal Pharmacopoeia (BHP) 1983 monograph
- **Recorded actions**: Antispasmodic, sedative, astringent
- **Indications**: Dysmenorrhea, threatened miscarriage, nervous conditions
- **Status**: Formally recognized in British herbal medicine; BHP monograph establishes official quality standards
- **Preparation**: Bark decoction, tincture, fluid extract

### German/Central European Naturopathic Practice
- **Tradition**: German naturopathic and herbal medicine (Naturheilkunde)
- **Dosing per Weiss** (German phytotherapy tradition):
  - 4–8 mL tincture (1:5), three to four times daily for dysmenorrhea
- **Status**: Widely used in practice despite no positive German Commission E monograph; Commission E absence reflects lack of review, not negative assessment

### Eastern European Folk Medicine (Kalina tradition)
- **Names**: "Kalina" (Eastern Europe — Poland, Russia, Ukraine)
- **Indications**: Menstrual irregularities, uterine spasms, folk treatment for miscarriage prevention
- **Parts used**: Bark, fruit
- **Ethnobotanical significance**: "Kalina" is deeply embedded in Slavic folk culture; extensively documented in ethnobotanical literature for reproductive and cardiovascular conditions

---

## Turkish Folk Medicine (Gilaburu tradition)

- **Common name in Turkey**: "Gilaburu"
- **Part used**: Fruit (berries/juice) — distinct from Western bark-based tradition
- **Primary indication**: Kidney and urinary stones (taş düşürücü = "stone-dropping")
- **Also used for**: Hypertension, diabetes, urinary tract infections
- **Regional concentration**: Kayseri region (Central Anatolia); considered a regional specialty
- **Preparation**: Fermented or fresh fruit juice; concentrated extract
- **Status**: The only V. opulus tradition with published human clinical data; Turkish urology literature (2019–2021) provides the sole RCT and retrospective study
- **Scientific validation**: The antiurolithiatic use is mechanistically supported by preclinical evidence (diuretic effect, chlorogenic acid content; Ilhan et al. 2014, PMID 25165481)

## Traditional Preparations

### Bark-Based (Western Tradition)

| Preparation | Method | Notes |
|-------------|--------|-------|
| Bark decoction | Simmer bark 10–15 min | 2–4 g per cup; three times daily (AHP 2000) |
| Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) | Maceration | 5–10 mL three times daily; most common commercial form |
| Fluid extract (1:1) | Percolation/maceration | 2–4 mL three times daily |
| Weiss tincture (1:5) | German tradition | 4–8 mL three to four times daily for dysmenorrhea |

### Fruit-Based (Turkish Tradition)
| Preparation | Method | Notes |
|-------------|--------|-------|
| Fresh fruit juice | Pressed | Gilaburu juice sold commercially in Turkey |
| Fermented/concentrated extract | Traditional processing | Used medicinally in Kayseri region |

## Key Therapeutic Themes Across Traditions

1. **Antispasmodic / smooth muscle relaxant**: Most consistent indication; appears across all traditions for uterine, urinary, and general smooth muscle spasm
2. **Dysmenorrhea**: Central therapeutic use in British, North American, and German traditions; supported by classical preclinical mechanistic work (Jarboe 1966, 1967)
3. **Threatened miscarriage**: Used historically to prevent uterine spasm and premature labor; requires caution re: dose and trimester
4. **Urinary/renal**: Kidney stone expulsion (Turkish gilaburu tradition); the only area with modern clinical trial data
5. **Hypertension**: Present in Eastern European folk use; supported by viopudial pharmacology (Nicholson 1972)
