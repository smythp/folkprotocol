# Licorice — Traditional Uses

*Glycyrrhiza glabra L. / G. uralensis Fisch. / G. inflata Bat. | Evidence as of 2026-02-28*

---

## Species and Regional Distribution

Three species are commercially used with overlapping but distinct traditional ranges:
- **Glycyrrhiza glabra L.** — European/Spanish licorice; the WHO and European Pharmacopoeia standard
- **Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch.** — Chinese licorice (Gan Cao); dominant in TCM
- **Glycyrrhiza inflata Bat.** — Used in some Asian traditions; covered under EMA monograph

---

## Traditional Medical Systems

### Chinese Medicine (TCM)

- **Name:** Gan Cao (甘草) — literally "sweet herb"
- **Species:** Primarily G. uralensis; G. inflata also used
- **Historical depth:** Among the most frequently prescribed herbs in TCM formulary; documented use spanning millennia
- **Classical uses:**
  - Harmonizing herb (调和药, tiáo hé yào): used in virtually all formula categories to moderate the actions of other herbs and reduce toxicity
  - Tonifying the Spleen and augmenting Qi
  - Moistening the Lungs and stopping cough
  - Clearing heat and relieving fire toxicity
  - Moderating spasms and alleviating pain
- **Preparation:** Decoction (water extract) as part of multi-herb formulas; honey-fried (Zhi Gan Cao, 炙甘草) form used specifically for tonifying Qi and nourishing the heart
- **Regulatory note:** EMA/HMPC monograph covers all three species including G. uralensis (2013)

### Ayurvedic Medicine

- **Name:** Yashtimadhu (यष्टिमधु) — literally "sweet stick"
- **Materia medica classification:** Rasayana (rejuvenating), Sita (cooling), Madhura (sweet taste and post-digestive effect)
- **Traditional uses:**
  - Respiratory: cough, bronchitis, hoarseness, sore throat
  - Gastric: ulcers, hyperacidity, inflammation of the digestive tract
  - Neurological: general tonic, adaptogen, cognitive support
  - Topical: skin disorders, wound healing, anti-inflammatory
  - Reproductive: used in preparations for both male and female health
- **Preparations:** Decoctions (kashaya), herbal ghee preparations (ghrita), powders (churna), medicated oils
- **Classical texts:** Referenced in Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita

### European Herbal Medicine

- **Regional name:** Liquorice, sweet root, Lakritze (German), Réglisse (French)
- **Historical record:**
  - Ancient Egypt: licorice extracts found in tombs, including Tutankhamun's tomb (~1300 BCE)
  - Ancient Greece: Theophrastus (c. 300 BCE) described medicinal uses for thirst and respiratory conditions
  - Medieval Europe: Hildegard von Bingen (12th century CE) documented licorice for stomach and liver complaints
  - 17th–19th century apothecary: widely used for coughs, ulcers, and as a laxative base
- **Traditional uses:**
  - Upper respiratory: cough, bronchitis, sore throat, phlegm clearance
  - Gastrointestinal: gastric ulcers, heartburn, constipation (laxative in combination)
  - Anti-inflammatory (general)
  - Adrenal support and "tonic" use
- **German Commission E:** Positive monograph for catarrh of upper respiratory tract and gastric/duodenal ulcers — one of earliest formal European regulatory approvals for licorice

### WHO Summary of Traditional Uses

Per WHO Monographs Volume 1 (1999):
- Respiratory disorders (asthma, bronchitis)
- Anti-inflammatory
- Anti-ulcer (gastric and duodenal)
- Laxative
- Spasmolytic
- Sore throat demulcent

| Preparation | Form | Traditional Dosing | Notes |

| Dried root decoction | Boiled water extract | 1–5 g root, 3× daily | WHO-referenced standard |
| Root powder | Oral capsule/tablet | Variable | Requires standardization to GA content |
| Licorice stick | Chewed directly | Traditional European/Middle Eastern | Variable GA delivery |

| Compound decoction (TCM) | Multi-herb formula | As per individual formula | Gan Cao as harmonizing agent |

| Topical paste/poultice | Applied to skin or mucosa | 1–5% concentration | Aphthous ulcers, skin inflammation |
| Herbal ghee (Ayurveda) | Medicated fat | Per practitioner | Complex preparations |

## Traditional Safety Awareness

Pre-modern traditions recognized limits:
- European folk medicine warnings against excessive licorice in persons with edema, swollen limbs, or heart complaints — consistent with what is now understood as pseudoaldosteronism
- TCM dosing of Gan Cao: typically 2–12 g/day as harmonizing agent; higher doses considered potentially problematic
- Traditional European apothecary texts note licorice as "heating" and not appropriate for those with excess heat or high blood pressure constitutions
