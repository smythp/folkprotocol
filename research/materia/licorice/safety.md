# Licorice — Safety

*Glycyrrhiza glabra L. / G. uralensis Fisch. / G. inflata Bat. | Evidence as of 2026-02-28*

---

## Primary Safety Concern: Pseudoaldosteronism

**Mechanism:** Glycyrrhetinic acid (GE) — the systemically active metabolite of glycyrrhizin — inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) in the kidney. This enzyme normally inactivates cortisol to cortisone. When inhibited, cortisol accumulates and activates mineralocorticoid receptors, producing effects indistinguishable from primary aldosteronism.

**Clinical manifestations (pseudoaldosteronism syndrome):**
- Hypertension
- Hypokalemia
- Sodium and water retention (edema)
- Metabolic alkalosis
- Suppression of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)
- Muscle weakness (from hypokalemia)
- In severe cases: rhabdomyolysis, paralysis, hypertensive emergencies, encephalopathy

**Reversibility:** All effects are reversible upon discontinuation.

## Dose Thresholds (Evidence-Based)

### NOAEL — Foundational Human Safety Study

**van Gelderen et al. 2000 (PMID 11125713)**
- Design: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 39 healthy women aged 19–40; 8 weeks
- Dose arms: 0 mg, 1 mg/kg, 2 mg/kg, 4 mg/kg glycyrrhizic acid (GA) per day
- **NOAEL: 2 mg/kg body weight/day** (no observable adverse effect level)
- **LOAEL: 4 mg/kg/day** (sodium retention, RAAS suppression observed)
- **Proposed ADI: 0.2 mg/kg/day** (applying 10× safety factor)
- Practical equivalent: ~12 mg GA/day for a 60 kg person (approximately 6 g licorice confectionery/day assuming 0.2% GA)
- This study is the foundational NOAEL cited by European regulatory bodies

### WHO/EMA Safety Threshold

- **≤100 mg GA/day** — maximum daily intake; applies to foods and supplements
- Source: WHO Monograph Volume 1 (1999); aligned with EMA/HMPC monograph (2013)

### Critical Update: Effects at the WHO Threshold

**Af Geijerstam et al. 2024 (PMID 38246526)**
- N = 28 healthy adults; randomized crossover; 2-week intervention periods
- Intervention: exactly 100 mg GA/day from commercial licorice candy
- Results:
  - Systolic home BP: **+3.1 mmHg** (95% CI: 0.8, 5.4 mmHg)
  - Renin: suppressed by **−30.0%** (95% CI: −56.7%, −3.3%)
  - Aldosterone: suppressed by **−45.1%** (95% CI: −61.5%, −28.7%)
- **Significance:** The 100 mg GA/day threshold itself produces measurable cardiovascular and hormonal effects. The WHO safety limit may be insufficiently protective; reassessment warranted.

### Additional Dose Benchmarks

- **≥100 mg GA/day**: Measurable BP and RAAS effects (Af Geijerstam 2024)
- **>400 mg glycyrrhetinic acid/day**: Consistently produces mineralocorticoid effects
- **50 g licorice/day (≈75 mg glycyrrhetinic acid) × 2 weeks**: Can cause significant BP rise
- **Licorice flavonoid fraction**: Does not raise blood pressure at therapeutic doses (Wu et al. 2024, meta-analysis of 8 RCTs, N = 541)

## Contraindications

The following are absolute or strong contraindications, as listed across EMA/HMPC monograph (2013) and German Commission E:

| Contraindication | Basis |
|---|---|
| Hypertension | Licorice raises systolic BP even at 100 mg GA/day |
| Hypokalemia | Licorice causes further potassium loss via mineralocorticoid effect |
| Diuretic use | Additive hypokalemia risk (loop diuretics, thiazides) |
| Liver cirrhosis | Impaired metabolism; risk of severe electrolyte disturbance |
| Cholestatic liver disorders | EMA/Commission E contraindication |
| Severe renal insufficiency | Reduced clearance; risk of accumulation and electrolyte crisis |
| Pregnancy | Potential for premature labor; EMA contraindication |

---

## Pregnancy and Lactation

**Pregnancy:**
- **Contraindicated** per EMA/HMPC and German Commission E
- Potential to trigger premature delivery
- WHO monograph notes safety concerns in pregnancy
- Avoid all whole-root licorice and GA-containing preparations during pregnancy

**Lactation:**
- No clinical data; WHO and EMA monographs do not establish safety in breastfeeding
- General principle: avoid GA-containing preparations; DGL status in lactation not specifically studied

---

## Duration Limits

- **Whole-root preparations:** Maximum 4 weeks continuous use without medical supervision (EMA/HMPC); Commission E recommends not beyond 4–6 weeks
- **DGL preparations:** Safer for prolonged GI use; lacks mineralocorticoid effects; no formal duration limit established for DGL specifically
- All clinical RCTs reporting safety: 7–60 days; no long-term safety data (>8 weeks) for licorice in clinical trials

## Drug Interactions

### Documented Interactions

| Drug Class | Mechanism | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Antihypertensives | Licorice raises blood pressure; may blunt drug effect or worsen control | Clinically significant in hypertensive patients on treatment |
| Corticosteroids | Additive potassium loss; GE potentiates cortisol effects (11β-HSD inhibition) | Enhanced steroid toxicity; electrolyte disturbance |
| Loop diuretics (furosemide) | Additive hypokalemia | Potentially severe hypokalemia |
| Thiazide diuretics | Additive hypokalemia | Potentially severe hypokalemia |
| Warfarin/anticoagulants | Enzyme interactions (glycyrrhizin affects hepatic enzymes); monitoring required | INR fluctuations |
| Cortisone acetate (Addison's) | Licorice increases cortisol availability by 11β-HSD2 inhibition | May reduce cortisone acetate dose requirement; also risk of over-cortisol exposure (Methlie 2011) |

### Proposed/Theoretical Interactions

| Drug Class | Mechanism | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|
| CYP3A4 substrates | In vitro data suggest licorice may affect CYP3A4 activity | In vitro only; clinical significance unclear |
| Hormonal contraceptives | Theoretical interaction via hormonal effects (testosterone suppression, estrogenic flavonoids) | Theoretical |

## Genotoxicity and Carcinogenicity

Per Isbrucker & Burdock 2006 (PMID 16884839) comprehensive review:
- Glycyrrhizin: **neither teratogenic nor mutagenic** at tested doses
- Anti-genotoxic potential observed under some conditions (preclinical)
- No carcinogenic signal identified in reviewed literature

---

## Epimerism Safety Note

For IV/injectable glycyrrhizin preparations (clinical/hospital use):
- 18α-glycyrrhizinate (magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate) has 74% lower adverse drug reaction rate than 18β compound
- RR = 0.26 (95% CI: 0.18–0.38, p < 0.00001; I² = 0%) across 24 RCTs, N = 2,757 (Chen et al. 2020)
- 18α form preferred where IV glycyrrhizin is used clinically

---

## DGL Safety Profile

Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) preparations:
- GA content reduced to <2–3% (vs 4–24% in raw root)
- Pseudoaldosteronism effects: eliminated or negligible
- Blood pressure and electrolyte effects: not observed in clinical trials of DGL preparations
- All three GutGard clinical trials (Raveendra 2012, Raj 2025, Puram 2013) reported well-tolerated safety comparable to placebo with no blood pressure or electrolyte adverse events
- Preferred formulation for long-term GI use

## Summary: Safety Decision Framework

| Preparation | Duration | Key Monitoring | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| DGL (deglycyrrhizinated) | Flexible; no mineralocorticoid concern | Routine | Most adults; preferred for GI use |
| Whole-root / standardized (GA-containing) | ≤4 weeks without supervision | BP, serum K⁺ | Healthy adults without listed contraindications |
| GA-containing, any dose | Any | BP, K⁺, renal function | Avoid in hypertension, hypokalemia, pregnancy, renal/hepatic impairment |
| >100 mg GA/day | Any | Cardiovascular effects expected even in healthy adults | Medical supervision required |
