# Licorice — Active Compounds

*Glycyrrhiza glabra L. / G. uralensis Fisch. / G. inflata Bat. | Evidence as of 2026-02-28*

---

## Primary Active Compound: Glycyrrhizin (Glycyrrhizic Acid)

**Chemical identity:** Glycyrrhizin (glycyrrhizic acid, GA) — a triterpene saponin; the principal bioactive compound and primary standardization marker across all regulatory monographs.

**Content in dried root:** 4–24% GA (dry basis); European Pharmacopoeia minimum standard is ≥4.0% (dry basis).

**Metabolic fate (critical for understanding clinical effects):**
- GA has negligible intact oral bioavailability — it is pre-systemically hydrolyzed to **glycyrrhetinic acid (GE)** by intestinal microflora before absorption
- GE is the systemically active form
- GE is 200–1,000× more potent as an 11β-HSD2 inhibitor than GA
- GE undergoes enterohepatic circulation; urinary excretion is very low (<1% of administered dose as GE + GE glucuronides in 24 hours)

### Pharmacokinetics of Glycyrrhetinic Acid (Key Human Data)

**Krähenbühl et al. 1994 (PMID 8126129) — dose-escalation PK study in healthy volunteers:**
- Doses tested: 500 mg, 1000 mg, 1500 mg 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (GRA)
- Half-life at 1000 mg: **11.5 ± 1.2 hours**
- Half-life at 1500 mg: **38.7 ± 10.5 hours** (dose-nonlinear; biphasic elimination at ≥1000 mg)
- PK pattern: biphasic elimination observed at doses ≥1000 mg
- Urinary excretion: <1% of administered dose (GRA + GRA glucuronides combined in 24h)
- Cmax and AUC increased proportionally with dose escalation
- Pharmacodynamics: higher GRA doses showed declining cortisone and increased cortisol/cortisone ratio — direct evidence of 11β-HSD inhibition in vivo
- Significance: Establishes enterohepatic circulation, long half-life at clinical doses, and dose-nonlinear PK; key for understanding accumulation risk

**Raggi et al. 1994 (PMID 8197226) — bioavailability comparison:**
- Significantly reduced bioavailability when glycyrrhizin is administered as licorice extract vs pure glycyrrhizin
- Matrix components in the extract inhibit intestinal absorption
- Explains why licorice extract shows lower and more variable clinical effects than isolated glycyrrhizin

**Isbrucker & Burdock 2006 (PMID 16884839) — comprehensive safety/PK review:**
- Confirms GA has no detectable intact oral bioavailability
- US glycyrrhizin consumption estimated at 0.027–3.6 mg/kg/day
- Proposed ADI: 0.015–0.229 mg glycyrrhizin/kg body weight/day
- GE is the bioactive, systemically available metabolite

### Epimerism: 18α vs 18β Glycyrrhizinate

Glycyrrhizin exists in two epimeric forms that differ significantly in safety:
- **18β-glycyrrhizinate** (compound glycyrrhizin) — the naturally occurring form; higher adverse drug reaction rate
- **18α-glycyrrhizinate** (magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate) — a pharmaceutical epimer used in injectable preparations

**Chen et al. 2020 (PMID 33312222) — meta-analysis of 24 RCTs (N = 2,757):**
- ADR rate: RR = 0.26 (95% CI: 0.18–0.38, p < 0.00001) favoring 18α epimer
- No heterogeneity (I² = 0%, p = 1.00)
- 18α-glycyrrhizinate has ~74% lower adverse drug reaction rate than 18β compound
- Clinically significant difference for injectable glycyrrhizin formulations

## Flavonoids

Licorice root contains a rich flavonoid profile distinct from glycyrrhizin, with different bioactivity and safety characteristics. DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice) preparations retain these compounds while removing GA.

### Key Flavonoids (G. glabra)

**Glabridin**
- Primary flavonoid in G. glabra
- Standardization marker for DGL GutGard preparations (≥3.5% w/w in GutGard® per Raj et al. 2025)
- Biological activities: estrogen receptor modulation, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial
- Acts locally in GI tract in DGL preparations; minimal systemic exposure expected

**Liquiritigenin**
- Flavanone; estrogenic activity via ERβ selective agonism
- Proposed mechanism for licorice hot flash effects (phytoestrogenic activity)
- Present in both G. glabra and G. uralensis

**Isoliquiritigenin**
- Chalcone precursor to liquiritigenin
- Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-cancer (preclinical); contributes to GI anti-inflammatory effects

**Lichalcone (lichalocone)**
- Present in G. inflata root
- Antimicrobial activity against H. pylori and other pathogens (preclinical)
- Anti-inflammatory via NF-κB pathway inhibition

**Glycyrrhizin-component note:** Wahab et al. 2021 (PMID 34961221) comprehensive review highlights isoliquiritigenin, liquiritigenin, lichalocone, and glabridin as the primary flavonoids with documented biological activities.

## Mechanism of Action by Compound Class

| Compound | Primary Mechanism | Clinical Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Glycyrrhetinic acid (GE) | 11β-HSD2 inhibition → cortisol accumulation → mineralocorticoid receptor activation | Pseudoaldosteronism (safety), cortisol augmentation (Addison's), testosterone suppression |
| GA (intact) | RAAS suppression (indirect via GE); anti-inflammatory (direct in vitro) | Blood pressure, electrolytes |
| Glabridin | ERα/β modulation; anti-inflammatory; H. pylori inhibition | GI protection, estrogen-related effects (DGL preparations) |
| Liquiritigenin | ERβ selective agonism | Hot flashes, hormonal effects |
| Isoliquiritigenin | NF-κB inhibition; antioxidant | Anti-inflammatory, GI protection |
| Lichalcone | Antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory | H. pylori (G. inflata); respiratory antimicrobial |

## Standardization Markers

| Marker | Standard | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Glycyrrhizic acid (GA) | ≥4.0% dry basis (Ph. Eur.); 4–24% typical range in root | All regulatory monographs; traditional preparations |
| Glabridin | ≥3.5% w/w | DGL GutGard® standardization (Raj et al. 2025) |
| Total flavonoids | ≥10% w/w | DGL GutGard® standardization (Raj et al. 2025) |
| GA in DGL | Reduced to <2–3% (vs 4–24% in raw root) | Confirms deglycyrrhizination; safety criterion |

## DGL vs Whole-Root Preparations

**Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice (DGL):**
- GA content: removed to <2–3%
- Retains flavonoid profile (glabridin, liquiritigenin, isoliquiritigenin, etc.)
- Lacks mineralocorticoid effects; preferred for long-term GI use
- Acts locally in GI tract; limited systemic exposure
- Approved clinical evidence: functional dyspepsia (Raveendra 2012), GERD (Raj 2025), H. pylori (Puram 2013)

**Whole-root preparations:**
- Full GA content present; full mineralocorticoid risk applies
- Both flavonoid and saponin profiles intact
- WHO-approved dosing: 1–5 g dried root 3× daily
- Duration limit: ≤4 weeks continuous use without supervision
- Blood pressure and electrolyte monitoring required for extended use
