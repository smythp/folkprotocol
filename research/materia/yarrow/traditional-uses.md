# Yarrow — Traditional Uses

*Achillea millefolium L.*

---

## Overview

Yarrow is one of the longest-documented medicinal plants in the Western herbal tradition, with evidence of use spanning millennia across European, North American Indigenous, and to a lesser extent Middle Eastern traditions. The genus name *Achillea* derives from the myth of Achilles applying yarrow to warriors' wounds at Troy — reflecting its ancient hemostatic reputation. The plant has both official Commission E approval and a robust ethnobotanical record.

---

## European Tradition

### Ancient and Medieval

- **Pre-Christian Europe / Classical antiquity:** Dioscórides (1st century CE, *De Materia Medica*) recorded yarrow for wound healing, hemorrhage, and as an anti-inflammatory poultice
- **Medieval European herbalism (10th–16th centuries):** Recorded in Hildegard of Bingen's *Physica* (12th century) and later in major herbals (Fuchs, Dodoens) for wounds, digestive complaints, and fevers
- **Common name "nosebleed plant"** reflects pan-European tradition of using fresh crushed leaves to stop nasal bleeding (direct hemostatic application)

### Early Modern European Herbalism (16th–19th centuries)

- **John Gerard's Herbal (1597):** Listed for stanching blood, wound healing, and treating "bloody flux" (dysentery)
- **Nicholas Culpeper's Complete Herbal (1652):** Classified yarrow under Venus; recommended for "all diseases of the blood" — fevers, wounds, menstrual irregularities, and liver complaints
- **Preparation:** Fresh herb juice, poultice, infusion, and tincture in wine

### 20th-Century Western Herbalism

- **Anglo-American eclectic tradition (late 19th – early 20th c.):** Yarrow featured in the *Eclectic Materia Medica* for fevers (diaphoretic use), hemorrhage, and cystitis
- **Contemporary Western herbalism:** Yarrow remains a primary remedy for:
  - Fevers (diaphoretic in hot infusion — promotes sweating)
  - Dysmenorrhea and menstrual irregularity (antispasmodic)
  - Digestive bitters (appetite and dyspepsia)
  - First-aid wound healing (styptic/hemostatic)
  - Urinary tract inflammation

## North American Indigenous Traditions

Yarrow (*A. millefolium*) grows across North America and was widely used by Indigenous peoples. Moerman's *Native American Ethnobotany* database records uses across more than 60 tribal nations.

### Documented Traditional Uses by Tradition

| Tradition | Use | Preparation |
|-----------|-----|-------------|
| Navajo/Diné | Analgesic, ceremonial medicine | Poultice; steam |
| Ojibwe | Headache, fever, cold symptoms | Infusion |
| Cheyenne | Skin irritations, insect bites | Poultice of fresh leaves |
| Cherokee | Fever, diaphoretic | Hot infusion |
| Iroquois (Haudenosaunee) | Fevers; expectorant for respiratory complaints | Infusion |
| Blackfoot | External wounds; antiseptic | Fresh leaf poultice |
| Thompson (Nlaka'pamux) | Stomachache, colds | Decoction |
| Cree | Analgesic, external antiseptic | Fresh plant/poultice |

**Cross-tradition patterns:**
1. Wound healing / hemostasis — the most universal Indigenous use
2. Fever management (diaphoretic effect)
3. Digestive complaints (stomachache, colic)
4. Respiratory complaints (colds, cough)

## Ayurvedic Tradition

Yarrow is not a primary Ayurvedic herb — it is not native to the Indian subcontinent. However, *Achillea millefolium* has been referenced in later Ayurvedic literature as an introduced medicinal and in Greco-Arabic (Unani) medicine, particularly in Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Central Asia:

- **Unani (Greco-Arabic) medicine:** Used as *Biranjasiph* — anti-inflammatory, febrifuge, diuretic, and emmenagogue
- **No classical Ayurvedic canon reference** (Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam) for *A. millefolium* specifically
- Regional *Achillea* species (e.g., *A. santolina*) are documented in Middle Eastern ethnomedicine with similar wound-healing and digestive indications

## Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)

*Achillea millefolium* is not a traditional TCM herb from classical Chinese pharmacopoeia. However:

- Chinese *Achillea* species (particularly *A. alpina* — 蓍草 shī cǎo) are documented in the *Bencao Gangmu* (Li Shizhen, 16th century) as a divination herb (for Yi Ching/I Ching stalks) with secondary medicinal uses
- European yarrow has entered Chinese traditional practice as an import and is used in some regional traditional Chinese medical systems, particularly in northwestern China
- **TCM categories that apply (where referenced):** Dispels Wind-Damp; cools Blood; hemostatic; benefits the Liver

*Note: Traditional use of A. millefolium in TCM is limited and secondary; it is fundamentally a European and North American traditional plant.*

## Traditional Preparations

### Internal

| Preparation | Method | Traditional Dosing |
|-------------|--------|--------------------|
| Hot infusion (tea) | 1–2 tsp dried herb/flower in 1 cup boiling water, steep 10–15 min | 1 cup, 3× daily |
| Cold infusion (bitter) | Dried herb steeped in cold water overnight | Taken before meals for appetite |
| Tincture (1:5, 25–40% alcohol) | Standard liquid extract | 2–4 mL, 3× daily (traditional guideline) |
| Fresh herb juice | Pressed from fresh aerial parts | Traditional hemostatic dose: small quantities |
| Powder in capsules | Dried ground herb | 500 mg/day used in modern trials |

### External

| Preparation | Use | Application |
|-------------|-----|-------------|
| Fresh leaf poultice | Wound, insect bite, nosebleed | Direct application |
| Strong infusion wash | Minor wounds, skin irritation | Topical rinse |
| Ointment / salve | Hemorrhoids, skin healing | Applied locally (5% extract used in 2023 RCT) |
| Compress | Bruises, sprains | Soaked cloth applied |
| Sitz bath | Perineal healing, vaginal complaints | Traditional European use |

## Traditional Safety Notes

- **Traditional contraindication in pregnancy:** Universally recorded across European and Indigenous traditions; emmenagogue properties were explicitly noted (property to provoke menstruation)
- **Asteraceae allergy cross-reactivity:** Recognized in traditional herbalism (avoid if sensitive to chamomile, ragweed, feverfew)
- **Nosebleed paradox:** The common name "nosebleed plant" refers to both using the herb to *stop* nosebleeds (hemostatic) and the traditional use of sniffing fresh leaves to *induce* nosebleeds (as a therapeutic "bleeding" technique) — a practice no longer in use
