# Yarrow — Safety

*Achillea millefolium L.*

---

## Overview

Yarrow has a well-established safety profile at conventional therapeutic doses. Six RCTs with doses up to 500 mg/day for up to 1 year reported no serious adverse events. The primary documented safety concern is **contact dermatitis** from sesquiterpene lactones. Clinically significant **drug interactions** exist (particularly with digoxin). Pregnancy use is universally contraindicated.

---

## Contraindications

### 1. Asteraceae (Compositae) Family Allergy

**Evidence level:** Well-documented; regulatory consensus (Commission E, EMA)
**Detail:** Cross-reactive with sesquiterpene lactones shared across Asteraceae — feverfew (*Tanacetum parthenium*), chrysanthemum, tansy, ragweed (*Ambrosia* spp.), chamomile (*Matricaria/Chamaemelum*), and echinacea.
**Prevalence:** Patch test data from dermatology clinics: approximately 1–2% of patients react to yarrow or Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactone mix.
**Action:** Absolute contraindication in known Asteraceae allergy. Careful history-taking required.

### 2. Pregnancy

**Evidence level:** Traditional contraindication across all traditions; regulatory consensus; experimental uterine stimulant activity
**Detail:**
- Commission E (1990): contraindicated in pregnancy
- EMA/HMPC monographs: no explicit age/pregnancy statement in summary, but clinical practice contraindication is universal
- Traditional use: universally listed as emmenagogue (provokes menstruation) in European, Indigenous North American, and Unani traditions
- Experimental data: uterine stimulant activity demonstrated in preclinical studies
- No human teratogenicity studies exist

**Action:** Absolute contraindication throughout pregnancy. Any use for menstrual regulation should be in consultation with a qualified practitioner.

### 3. Concentrated Essential Oil — Internal Use

**Evidence level:** Toxicological data (camphor, thujone)
**Detail:**
- **Camphor:** 2g harmful in adults; 4g potentially fatal; lethal dose for children 0.5–1g; fatal dose range 50–500 mg/kg
- **Alpha-thujone:** More neurotoxic than beta-thujone; LD50 45 mg/kg (mice); optimal daily intake estimated 3–7 mg/day; high doses cause convulsions

*Note: Risks apply to concentrated essential oil, not to therapeutic infusions or dried herb extracts at conventional doses. Steam distillation and boiling reduce alpha-thujone content in infusions.*

**Action:** Yarrow essential oil should not be taken internally. For aromatherapy and external use, standard dilution protocols apply.

## Drug Interactions

### High Clinical Risk

| Drug | Mechanism | Effect | Risk Level |
|------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| **Digoxin** | Quercetin inhibits P-glycoprotein (P-gp/ABCB1) | **413% ↑ digoxin AUC** | **HIGH — documented in human PK study** |
| **Warfarin** | CYP2C9 inhibition (chamazulene) + possible antiplatelet effects | Altered anticoagulant effect; increased bleeding risk | HIGH |
| **Cyclosporine** | CYP3A4 inhibition (chamazulene) | ↑ immunosuppressant levels | HIGH |
| **Topotecan** | P-gp inhibition (quercetin) | ↑ cytotoxic drug exposure | HIGH — co-administration to be avoided |

### Moderate Clinical Risk

| Drug | Mechanism | Effect | Risk Level |
|------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Erythromycin | CYP3A4 inhibition | ↑ antibiotic levels | Moderate |
| Diazepam / benzodiazepines | CYP3A4 inhibition | ↑ sedative effect | Moderate |
| Antiplatelet agents (aspirin, clopidogrel) | Pharmacodynamic synergy | Increased bleeding risk | Moderate |
| Antihypertensives | Pharmacodynamic synergy | Additive blood pressure lowering | Moderate |
| CYP1A2 substrates (theophylline, caffeine) | CYP1A2 inhibition (chamazulene) | ↑ substrate levels | Moderate |
| CYP2D6 substrates (codeine, tramadol, some antidepressants) | CYP2D6 inhibition (chamazulene) | Altered drug metabolism | Moderate |
| Antihistamines | Quercetin-mediated | Reduced antihistamine effect | Low–Moderate |
| Immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, sirolimus) | CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition | ↑ immunosuppressant levels | Moderate |

### Combination Herbs

| Combination | Risk | Basis |
|------------|------|-------|
| **Hawthorn (*Crataegus*)** | **Contraindicated — toxicity risk** | Documented in pharmacological literature |

**Mechanism of CYP interactions:** Chamazulene is the primary inhibitor (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP3A4). CYP inhibition is in vitro-confirmed; in vivo magnitude depends on chamazulene content of preparation (chemotype-dependent) and dose. The digoxin DDI is human PK study-confirmed.

## Adverse Effects

### Contact Dermatitis (Primary Concern)

**Evidence level:** Well documented in humans — case reports, occupational studies, patch test data
**Prevalence:** ~1–2% of patients in dermatology patch-test series react to yarrow or Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactone mix
**Sensitizer:** Alpha-peroxyachifolid (principal contact allergen; guaianolide sesquiterpene lactone)
**Routes:** Topical application (primary), occupational handling, cosmetic products
**Cross-reactivity:** Feverfew, chrysanthemum, tansy, ragweed, chamomile, mugwort — all Asteraceae with shared sesquiterpene lactone antigens

### Photosensitization

**Evidence level:** Documented in Commission E; case reports
**Detail:** Rare; reported with topical application and sun exposure. Furocoumarins may contribute.

### Gastrointestinal

**Evidence level:** Rare; from clinical trials
**Detail:** At therapeutic doses in clinical trials, GI adverse events were not significant. At high essential oil doses, GI complaints reported.

### Reproductive (Animal Data Only)

**Evidence level:** Animal study only — not observed at therapeutic doses
**Detail:** High doses (800 mg/kg body weight) in male rats caused transitory seminiferous tubule damage and antifertility effects; effects were reversible. Not relevant to therapeutic doses (500 mg/day = ~7 mg/kg for 70 kg adult).

## Pregnancy and Lactation

### Pregnancy

**Recommendation: CONTRAINDICATED**

All regulatory sources (Commission E, EMA) and traditional authorities contraindicate yarrow during pregnancy. Experimental uterotonic/emmenagogue activity is documented. No human safety data exists.

### Lactation

**Recommendation: AVOID (insufficient data)**

Commission E and EMA monographs do not explicitly address lactation, but conservative practice contraindicates use. No human lactation safety data exists. Lipophilic constituents (essential oil components) may transfer to breast milk.

## Duration Limits (Regulatory)

| Indication | Maximum Duration (Without Medical Supervision) | Source |
|-----------|-----------------------------------------------|--------|
| Appetite loss, GI complaints | **2 weeks** | EMA/HMPC monograph |
| Wound healing, menstrual spasm | **1 week** | EMA/HMPC monograph |
| Any indication (Commission E) | Not specified; implied short-term | Commission E (1990) |

**Clinical trial durations without serious adverse events:**
- 10 days (hemorrhoids topical)
- 14 days (oral mucositis mouthwash)
- 90 days (500 mg/day oral, T2D)
- 1 year (250–500 mg/day oral, MS add-on therapy)

| Narrow therapeutic index drug users (digoxin, warfarin, cyclosporine) | Contraindicated without medical supervision |
| Pre-surgery | Discontinue ≥2 weeks pre-operatively (antiplatelet activity) |

## Overall Safety Assessment

At conventional therapeutic doses (2–4 g dried herb as infusion × 3/day; 250–500 mg/day encapsulated extract):

- **Generally well-tolerated:** 6 RCTs reported no serious adverse events
- **Primary risk:** Contact sensitization in topically exposed individuals; Asteraceae-allergic individuals
- **Key DDI risk:** Quercetin-mediated digoxin interaction (413% AUC increase) is clinically confirmed
- **Long-term safety:** 1 year of 250–500 mg/day in MS trial with no serious adverse events — most reassuring available data
- **Essential oil:** Substantially different risk profile; internal use contraindicated

*Source: Far et al. 2023 (PMID 38076118); Commission E 1990; EMA/HMPC 2011; clinical trial safety data from all 6 identified RCTs*
