# 70 — Practice Strain Field: Hemorrhoids, Pregnancy and Postpartum

## How this field was reconstructed

This file reconstructs a practice-strain field from the six upstream maps
(10 through 60) covered by the input digest above, plus the commission
(00). No new source was opened, searched, or invented; every claim below
cites an existing map-local source ID, or names the map and section
directly when the point is a map-level access failure or negative-space
note with no local ID. An internal inventory of every concrete action
across the six maps was built first, including actions embedded inside
source summaries or offered with an implausible folk rationale, then
grouped into broad strains by shared operative move or native logic
rather than by source domain, ingredient, or verdict.

## Broad practice strains

### 1. Warm-water sitz bathing

Operative move: soaking the perianal area in warm water on the shared
premise that local water contact helps, independent of which mechanism a
given source assigns to it.

Plain warm water is treated as sufficient alone by a hospital
patient-education page (104°F, 15–20 minutes) [40:CW6] and a nonprofit
postpartum educator (10–20 minutes, additives optional) [60:PF10]; forum
and doula material frames the same soak as a near-universal first move
with a folk-mechanistic rationale [10:RF2] or a "self-care ritual"
[60:PF8]. Herbal-infusion variants add a brewed blend to the bathwater: a
midwife-herbalist's postpartum protocol (comfrey, calendula, yarrow,
myrrh, witch hazel, sea salt, steeped 30 minutes) [60:PF2], a nearly
identical witch-hazel-forward blend from a herbalism-education blog
[60:PF3], and Ayurvedic practitioners converging on Triphala- or
turmeric-based sitz baths as one part of a broader kit [60:PF6]. TCM
treats bath temperature as diagnostic rather than fixed — cool for
damp-heat/heat patterns, warm only for qi-stagnation [20:TR4] — inverting
the "warm is default" framing found everywhere else in this strain.

Whether Epsom salt adds anything to plain water is unresolved everywhere
it is raised: a seller claims it "stop[s] hemorrhoids at the source by
relieving constipation" [40:CW5], a hospital page states "salts, oils and
other substances may cause inflammation" [40:CW6], forum posters split
between finding it inert-but-pleasant [10:RF6] and reporting both
momentary relief and skin irritation from the same practice with no
within-person comparison [50:EX7], and two practitioner sources call it
optional, no-mechanism "spa vibes" [60:PF8] or provider-suggested rather
than integral [60:PF10]. No source in any map names a mechanism for
"warm-then-cold contrast," or reports it as a recognized practice at all
(10-reddit-forums §5; 40-commercial-web §4; 60-practitioner-folk-web §6).
The one controlled trial of sitz bathing located studied
post-hemorrhoidectomy patients, not conservative or pregnancy-related
use, and found no pain or analgesic-use benefit over standard care
[30:PR9].

Footprint: broad, present in all six maps — lay forum, hospital and
nonprofit patient education, DTC seller copy, practitioner blogs, and one
population-mismatched RCT. The Epsom-salt question is disputed
everywhere it appears; the herbal-infusion variant is practitioner-only;
the TCM temperature-matching logic is confined to that tradition.

### 2. Witch hazel and other cold/astringent topical contact

Operative move: cooling or astringent contact between a material and the
tissue surface, held there by pad, wipe, or frozen application, rather
than absorbed or ingested.

Witch hazel is the dominant single active across formats: branded pads
(50% solution) [40:CW2], a wipe line marketed by name for pregnancy
[40:CW1], refrigerable 50% foam [40:CW3], and the unbranded DIY
"padsicle" (witch hazel- and sometimes aloe/arnica-soaked pad, frozen
ahead of delivery), promoted by a birth-worker blogger [40:CW9] and
described as a batched, pre-made forum practice [10:RF5]. Frozen witch
hazel pads sit alongside plain ice and instant-cold pads as a stacked
kit rather than one remedy [10:RF5][10:RF12]. An older personal-herbalist
blog (2008) pairs witch hazel with yarrow topically, without the
tissue-tone theory used elsewhere [60:PF5]. A multi-herb salve (olive
oil, beeswax, witch hazel, plantain, yarrow, calendula) is marketed
explicitly as not a steroid, safe for continuous pregnancy/postpartum use
[40:CW4] — a "safe because it isn't a drug" narrative distinct from the
cooling-contact framing above it. A secondary pharmacological review
attributes the plant's hemorrhoid use to Native American bark-decoction
practice without naming a people or primary record, a vagueness it
repeats rather than resolves [20:TR9]. One forum thread addresses
lactation directly, treating witch hazel as compatible with breastfeeding
while noting a recurrence-across-pregnancies pattern [10:RF10].

Witch hazel carries high recommendation volume but individual negative
reports (stinging, "did not work," abandoned quickly) sit beside strongly
positive ones in the same community without reconciliation [10:RF7]. No
current systematic review of topical OTC treatments including witch
hazel exists; the relevant Cochrane review was withdrawn for failing
current methodological standards, with no plans to revise [30:PR11].

Footprint: broad across five maps and present in lay pregnancy
crowd-sourcing as stable kit vocabulary [50:EX10][50:EX11]; commercially
the single most repeated active in the category; evidentially unreviewed
rather than validated or refuted.

### 3. Topical pharmacological actives, OTC through prescription

Operative move: applying a specific drug-class active for anesthetic,
vasoconstrictive, or anti-inflammatory effect, chosen by named ingredient,
escalating to prescription strength when OTC measures plateau.

One brand's own product line splits by active — pramoxine alone;
lidocaine plus phenylephrine, framed as "rapid"/"instant"; and witch
hazel, framed as gentlest and the only line named for pregnancy [40:CW1].
Pregnancy forum advice makes the same choice explicitly, favoring a
lidocaine-only product over a hydrocortisone-containing combination and
naming a prescription ladder (Anusol suppositories, Scheriproct,
Cathejell, EMLA) once OTC measures plateau [10:RF9][10:RF12]. Steroid
creams are recommended for short-term relief but flagged, by posters and
a self-identified physician-poster in the same thread, for thinning
perianal skin with continued use, with about a week named as the
boundary [10:RF9].

Footprint: narrow but ingredient-specific, drawn from forum advice and
one commercial product line, with no equivalent pregnancy guidance
located in the traditional, published-research, experimental, or
practitioner maps. The research footprint is negative: the Cochrane
review most relevant to comparing these actives was withdrawn as failing
current standards [30:PR11]; a 2020 European surgical-society guideline
exists, but this pass could not confirm what it says about conservative
topical care in pregnancy [30:PR10].

### 4. Oral venotonic and phlebotonic compounds

Operative move: taking a plant flavonoid or related compound by mouth on
the belief that it tones veins or reduces capillary permeability,
treated as a systemic complement to topical care.

Outside the US, micronized diosmin 90%/hesperidin 10% (branded Daflon)
is sold with an acute-flare tapering schedule [40:CW11]; two open-label,
uncontrolled pregnancy trials test related compounds — diosmin/hesperidin
tapered from 8 weeks before to 4 weeks after delivery (n=50, 66% relief
by day 4) [30:PR4], and oral Euphorbia prostrata plus a topical cream for
14 days (n=100, up to 100% relief on some domains by 8 weeks) [30:PR5] —
neither controlled. A Cochrane review of oral phlebotonics generally (24
RCTs, non-pregnant) found benefit for pruritus, bleeding, discharge, and
overall symptoms, but not pain [30:PR6]. Horse chestnut is the second
major compound: a Cochrane review finds it reduces leg pain/volume in
general venous insufficiency, not hemorrhoid-specific and with no
pregnant participants [30:PR7]; forum posters report individual
enthusiasm [10:RF7]; a five-herb internal formula (horse chestnut, red
root, Oregon grape, yarrow, ginger) treats hemorrhoids as a tissue-tone
problem needing internal astringents [60:PF1]; a herbalist-botanist's
reference database gives the aescin mechanism and is the only source in
any map stating an explicit pregnancy/lactation contraindication for it
[60:PF4]; a reader-remedy column lists horse chestnut extract and rutin
among submissions held against its own stated skepticism [60:PF9].
Self-tracking communities combine these compounds into self-chosen
stacks at self-titrated doses diverging from any single label, assembly
increasingly attributed to a generative-AI query rather than peer
citation [50:EX5][50:EX6]; one recovery narrative credits an untested
order of operations (massage, then horse chestnut cream, with lubricant)
after the same cream alone reportedly failed earlier [50:EX5]; a
solicitation for experience with four named venotonic compounds received
zero replies [50:EX2]. A distinct, less-conflated venotonic plant,
butcher's broom, is attested by name across Mediterranean, Balkan, and
Levantine folk medicine [20:TR10]; horse chestnut's own historical record
is dated to 1800 by an uncorroborated commercial source [20:TR11]. A
narrative review surveys this same herbal literature while flagging
standardization and clinical validation as unresolved [30:PR8].

Horse chestnut's pregnancy contraindication [60:PF4] sits beside
pregnancy trials of the differently-sourced compounds diosmin and
Euphorbia [30:PR4][30:PR5] — an absence of horse-chestnut-specific
pregnancy data, not a contradiction. A widely repeated modern review
misattributes horse chestnut, a Balkan-European species, to Traditional
Chinese Medicine [20:TR12].

Footprint: broad across all six maps; pregnancy trial evidence is
open-label and uncontrolled; self-titrated stacking is confined to
non-pregnancy self-tracking forums.

### 5. Bowel and stool management to reduce straining

Operative move: managing stool consistency and defecation to prevent
straining, treated as a lane distinct from direct symptom relief.

Forum advice recurs on fiber, water, stool softener, prune juice, and
lactulose (10-reddit-forums §3). The only RCT testing a
straining-reduction bundle randomized pregnant women before 12 weeks'
gestation to structured dietary/behavioral counseling (fluids, fiber,
exercise, defecation technique) versus standard care, finding a large
reduction in postpartum hemorrhoid rate (RR 0.38) [30:PR3], building on
the same group's cohort finding that straining and delivery-related
mechanical load predict perianal disease [30:PR2]. A harm-reduction
community names opioid- and kratom-induced constipation as a
mechanistically distinct cause, met with a standard countermeasure kit
(magnesium, osmotic laxatives, psyllium, hydration) [50:EX3]. Ayurvedic
practitioners converge on a high-fiber, low-spice diet as part of a
broader kit [60:PF6]. Unani medicine frames evacuation itself, via
laxatives, guggul, and linseed, as the primary treatment rather than a
preventive adjunct, on a humoral rationale [20:TR6][20:TR7].

A forum thread and an experimental-community thread each raise, without
resolving, whether hemorrhoids require constipation at all, given cases
where they appear without it [10:RF11][50:EX8].

Footprint: broad across forum, published-research, experimental, and
practitioner/traditional material; absent as a distinct category from
the commercial map, where iron marketing is the closest adjacent claim
(strain 6); the Unani variant treats evacuation as curative, a different
logic from the modern behavioral-bundle framing.

### 6. Iron intake adjustment

Operative move: changing the form, dose, or timing of iron
supplementation to avoid the constipation it is believed to trigger,
rather than treating hemorrhoid symptoms directly.

A DTC prenatal brand markets a chelated form (ferrous bisglycinate) as
causing "fewer gastrointestinal upsets" [40:CW10]; a midwife-herbalist
separately recommends 27 mg specifically in chelate form as
non-constipating, without a dosing schedule [60:PF11]; a forum poster
describes moving her dose timing after tracing a
constipation-to-hemorrhoid chain to iron [10:RF4]; a general anemia
community discusses switching from daily to alternate-day dosing on the
belief that it improves absorption — the same self-titration logic
applied to tolerance rather than hemorrhoid onset [50:EX9].

Footprint: narrow and specific — present in forum, commercial,
experimental, and practitioner material but negative in both the
traditional and published-research maps, which explicitly found no
source giving iron a causal or management role at all (20-traditional
§4; 30-published-research §5).

### 7. Positional and postural offloading

Operative move: changing body position or toileting behavior to reduce
local venous or mechanical pressure, independent of stool management.

Left side-lying (fetal position) to relieve pressure on rectal veins
recurs across pregnancy forums [10:RF1][50:EX10] and two doula-practice
sources, one stating the rationale explicitly [60:PF7][60:PF8]; avoiding
prolonged toilet sitting appears alongside it [60:PF7]. Squatty-potty-style
posture elevation is praised generally as reducing strain, but one forum
poster frames a negative experience as explicit minority pushback against
described widespread community enthusiasm [10:RF8]. In strength-training
and PED-adjacent forums, heavy squats and deadlifts (Valsalva against a
closed glottis) are named as a direct cause, with posters substituting
leg extensions and curls on training days [50:EX12].

Footprint: narrow — forum, experimental, and practitioner material only;
both the traditional and published-research maps explicitly found no
trace of any named body position or toilet-sitting duration as a
hemorrhoid practice (20-traditional §4; 30-published-research §6). The
squatty-potty claim is internally contested; the others are not.

### 8. Diagnosis-matched traditional treatment systems

Operative move: diagnosing which named type, pattern, or humoral state a
case belongs to, then selecting the remedy, format, or escalation stage
matched to that diagnosis, rather than one fixed technique for every
case.

Ayurveda names six Arshas types (Vataja, Pittaja, Kaphaja, Raktaja,
Sannipataja, Sahaja), each with distinct appearance, discharge, and pain
quality, attributed to incompatible food combinations, prolonged
sitting, suppressed urges, and excess sexual activity, with curability
governed by anatomical location [20:TR1]. Treatment is staged by failure
of the prior stage: Bheshaja (medicine/diet), Kshara (caustic paste),
Agni Karma (thermal cautery), Shastra (surgical excision) [20:TR2]. A
separate twelve-week postpartum regimen, Sutika Paricharya, governs
tissue restoration and digestive-fire support generally, without naming
hemorrhoids specifically [20:TR3]. Eight independently named Ayurvedic
practitioners answering one reader converge on a common kit (Triphala
churna, Pilex, Kanchanara Guggulu internally; Jatyadi taila and
Triphala- or turmeric-based sitz baths externally; diet), varying only
in explanatory depth [60:PF6]. TCM differentiates zhì into at least four
patterns — damp-heat, large-intestine heat, qi-and-blood stagnation, and
spleen-qi sinking — each matched to its own herbal formula, bathing
temperature, and, for sinking qi, moxibustion at Baihui [20:TR4]; a
still-manufactured patent ointment, Ma Ying Long, applies the
damp-heat/toxin model directly to topical practice [20:TR5]. Unani
medicine frames causation as pooled melancholic humor in a
constitutionally predisposed person, treated by evacuation first
(laxatives, guggul, linseed) and escalating through leeching and
venesection to ligation and surgical cutting only in advanced cases, per
secondary literature on Avicenna's Canon not opened directly
[20:TR6][20:TR7].

None of these frameworks maps onto a hormonal or mechanical
venous-pressure account, and none frames pregnancy hemorrhoids as
distinct from a general aggravation of the underlying category
(20-traditional §4). A modern cross-tradition review misattributes horse
chestnut, a Balkan-European plant, to Traditional Chinese Medicine — a
documented flattening of exactly the kind this strain's boundaries are
meant to preserve against [20:TR12]. A published-research review calls
TCM's own mechanism literature scarce even on its own terms [30:PR12].

Footprint: broad within the traditional map (its majority content) with
a partial echo in one practitioner-web source [60:PF6]; absent as an
organizing logic from the forum, commercial, and experimental maps, and
present in the published-research map only as reviewed-not-tested
background.

### 9. Direct topical folk-material application

Operative move: applying a specific, often single, material substance
directly to the tissue on a locally specific rationale (drawing,
poulticing, or plain contact), distinct from both the branded
cooling-contact family and pharmaceutical actives.

The Babylonian Talmud (Avodah Zarah 28a–29a) records site-specific
remedies attributed to named sages, offered as sequential alternatives
rather than one formula — for the lower crevice, melted fat from a goat
that has not given birth, or ashes of shade-dried pumpkin leaves;
elsewhere, a bramble kernel applied "crevice against crevice," snail
shells, and an oil-and-wax dressing on worn cloth — framed in the text
as things reported to have worked for specific individuals, not a
general prescription [20:TR8]. A decades-running consumer-health column
has collected reader-submitted remedies including banana peel, rutabaga
poultice, and blackstrap molasses, explicitly separated from the
column's own stated skepticism and printed alongside reports of remedies
that failed [60:PF9]. A forum thread records granulated sugar applied to
a prolapse to draw water and reduce swelling, attributed in-thread to a
self-identified doctor-poster ("absorbs water in the prolapse and
shrinks it") [10:RF12].

Footprint: sparse and cross-map only by coincidence of category, not
shared source type — one classical text, one long-running lay column,
one forum anecdote. No published-research or commercial-web source
engages any of these specific materials.

### 10. Mechanical pressure-redistribution devices

Operative move: using a shaped seating device to redistribute pressure
away from the affected tissue while sitting.

A marketplace listing sells a "PT ENDORSED" donut cushion as a single
SKU across unrelated causes — hemorrhoids, coccyx pain, "prostate,"
postpartum, pressure sores — with no named practitioner or study behind
the credential-flavored branding and no pregnancy-specific
differentiation [40:CW12]. The same device is recommended in some forum
threads as pressure relief and blamed in others for making external
hemorrhoids worse, on the reasoning that the hemorrhoid sits lower than
the cushion's seat so pressure concentrates rather than distributes
[10:RF3]; the identical disagreement recurs independently on Mumsnet,
where a responder pushes back on the original poster's own donut-pillow
use in a case that later escalated to prescription treatment [10:RF12].

Footprint: narrow — forum and commercial maps only; no traditional,
published-research, experimental, or practitioner-web source engages
this device.

## Unclustered directions

**Manual and osmotic reduction of a protruding hemorrhoid.** One poster
describes physically pushing a hemorrhoid back inside after a sitz bath
and cream application [10:RF1]; a separate thread describes granulated
sugar drawing water from a prolapse to shrink it [10:RF12]. Both aim at
direct mechanical reduction of an already-protruding mass rather than
general symptom relief, tissue toning, or prevention, which is why
neither joins the bathing, topical-contact, or folk-material strains
above cleanly. Footprint: one map (10-reddit-forums), two accounts, no
corroboration or discussion found elsewhere.

**Rectal drug administration as a mechanical risk factor.** A
research-chemical community thread asks whether "boofing" (rectal drug
administration) is affected by existing hemorrhoids, treating the
practice as a separate mechanical stressor entangled with hemorrhoid
symptoms rather than proposing a treatment [50:EX4]. This does not fit
any treatment strain because it is a risk-awareness question about a
route of exposure, not a remedy. Footprint: one map
(50-experimental-communities), one thread, no visible reply.

**Explicit comparative tier-ranking as a personal decision practice.** A
rationalist-adjacent forum post ranks treatments the poster had
personally experienced into stated tiers ("Top Tier," "Mid Tier," "Avoid
Tier") by tradeoffs (effectiveness vs. pain vs. recurrence vs.
complication risk), then separately names prevention as "the most
actionable" category [50:EX1]. This is a practice of organizing and
comparing treatment choices, not a treatment itself, and no other source
in any map performs this kind of explicit labeled ranking. Footprint:
one map (50-experimental-communities), one post, one community.

## Coverage and boundary note

Negative space is kept as negative space rather than converted into a
practice. No tradition, study, or community discussion in any of the six
maps names venous pressure as a competing model to straining in its own
terms (20-traditional §4; 50-experimental-communities §5); the
commissioner's own "hemorrhoids without constipation" question surfaces
as a live, unanswered question inside forum and experimental material
itself [10:RF11][50:EX8]. No source in any map names a mechanism for
warm-then-cold contrast bathing or treats it as a recognized practice
(10-reddit-forums §5; 40-commercial-web §4; 60-practitioner-folk-web
§6).

Access failures bound what could be represented. All Reddit content in
the forum and experimental-communities maps is search-snippet-only,
since Reddit's domain blocked direct fetch in both passes
(10-reddit-forums §1; 50-experimental-communities §1). Amazon customer
reviews did not render, so customer-reported product failures are
absent from the commercial map's picture of the cushion and pad markets
(40-commercial-web §6). Several traditional sources rest on secondary
literature only or could not be opened at all — Avicenna's Canon, a
Sushruta Samhita review PDF, and an Ayurvedic pregnancy-constipation
source among them (20-traditional §5). PubMed pages were blocked by a
cookie wall throughout the published-research pass, which relied on
Europe PMC abstracts instead, and a 2024 ASCRS guideline and the ESCP
guideline's recommendation tables could not be retrieved in usable form
(30-published-research §1, §6); that map's own epidemiological anchor is
itself the subject of an unretrieved published dispute [30:PR1]. One
search-engine summary named a 2023 Cochrane review of hydrocortisone by
"Albuquerque et al." that could not be verified in Europe PMC and is
treated as a confabulation, not a source — named here only so it is not
mistaken for an omitted citation (30-published-research §1). Searches
for African, Latin American/Caribbean, Nordic, and Korean
hemorrhoid-specific lineages, and for a Wise Woman-tradition or
Appalachian-specific citation, returned nothing meeting the maps' own
sourcing standard and are not represented here (20-traditional §1;
60-practitioner-folk-web §6).

The commercial map also documents content that is not itself a practice:
a seller-facing market-sizing article naming the incumbent brand set
without patient-facing claims [40:CW7], and an undisclosed self-referral
from a competitor-name buying guide to the same site's own product line
[40:CW8] — kept out of the strains above since neither describes a thing
done to a body, named here rather than dropped silently.

This pass did not rank, score, or judge any strain, manifestation, or
dispute as safe, effective, suitable, or ready. The sitz-bath, Epsom-salt,
witch hazel, iron-form, and horse-chestnut-pregnancy questions the
commission opened remain exactly as open, contested, or unaddressed as
the six maps left them.
