# 60 — Practitioner and Folk-Web Map: Hemorrhoids, Pregnancy and Postpartum

Commission: hemorrhoids-v1. Scope: practicing herbalists, community educators, household
remedy archives, regional folk sources, newsletters, and independent sites outside Reddit,
academia, and storefronts. Map of practice and reasoning, not a verdict. Access date for all
sources unless noted: 2026-09-21.

Routes: named-practitioner queries (Aviva Romm, Christopher Hobbs, Susun Weed), formula and

## 2. Vocabulary and conceptual terrain

Western herbalist vocabulary centers on tissue "tone": hemorrhoids as a "relaxed tissue
state" corrected by astringents that "tighten up and tonify" [PF1], and on venous mechanics —
"portal vein" congestion, "venous circulation," aescin as the vein-toning constituent of horse
chestnut [PF1] [PF4]. Midwife-herbalist and doula material uses a softer, outcome-first
register: baths that "shrink hemorrhoids" [PF2], sitz baths as a "self-care ritual" with
Epsom salt for "extra spa vibes" [PF8] — mechanism is asserted less often than in the
clinical-herbalist register. Ayurveda supplies an entirely separate frame: hemorrhoids as
Arsha, driven by deranged Agni (digestive fire) producing Ama (toxins) that vitiate the three
doshas, chiefly Vata and Pitta [PF6] — a causal vocabulary with no analog in the Western
material reached here. "Padsicle," "peri-rinse," and "sitz" (German, "to sit") recur as
household/procedural vocabulary across the doula and postpartum-education sources [PF7] [PF8]
[PF10].

## 3. Major practice lineages

**A. Western clinical/energetic astringent-formula lineage.** A five-herb internal formula
(horse chestnut, red root, Oregon grape, yarrow, ginger) is built on the premise that
hemorrhoids are a tissue-tone problem requiring astringents rather than only topical relief
[PF1]. Christopher Hobbs' reference entry supplies the pharmacological version of the same
horse-chestnut logic — aescin reduces capillary permeability and increases venous tone — and
is the only source in this pass to state a pregnancy/lactation contraindication for it
explicitly [PF4]. An older personal-herbalist blog shows the same witch-hazel/yarrow pairing
applied topically rather than internally, without the tissue-tone theory [PF5].

**B. Midwife-herbalist perinatal bathing lineage.** A physician-midwife's postpartum herb-bath
protocol (comfrey, calendula, yarrow, myrrh, witch hazel, sea salt, steeped 30 minutes) is
offered explicitly for hemorrhoids alongside perineal tearing, brewed as a strong infusion and
added to bathwater [PF2]. A herbalism-education blog gives a near-identical
witch-hazel-forward blend with the same steep-and-strain method, but frames witch hazel's
hemorrhoid effect in general astringent terms rather than a specific mechanism [PF3].

**C. Doula/birth-worker positional and toilet-habit lineage.** Distinct from herbal treatment:
left side-lying (described as fetal position) to relieve pressure on rectal veins, avoiding
prolonged toilet sitting, and wet wipes over paper [PF7]. A second independent doula practice
gives the same left-side and warm-sitz-bath advice but frames Epsom salt only as adding "spa
vibes" with no mechanism claimed [PF8] — see §4. A nonprofit postpartum educator's protocol
treats added Epsom salt or herbs as optional and "provider-suggested" rather than integral to
the practice [PF10].

**D. Ayurvedic Arsha lineage.** Eight independently named, degree-holding practitioners
answering one reader's question converge on a common kit — Triphala churna, Pilex, Kanchanara
Guggulu internally; Jatyadi taila and Triphala or turmeric sitz baths externally; a
high-fiber, low-spice diet — with variation only in explanatory depth, not substance [PF6].

**E. Reader-remedy exchange lineage.** A pharmacologist/medical-anthropologist column has
collected decades of reader-submitted remedies (banana peel, rutabaga poultice, blackstrap
molasses, rutin, horse chestnut extract) and explicitly separates these from its own
skepticism, stating plainly when no evidence exists and printing reports of remedies that
failed [PF9].

**F. Iron-form-and-schedule lever.** The same midwife-herbalist source recommends 27 mg of
iron specifically in chelate form as non-constipating, without giving a dosing schedule or
timing relative to food or vitamin C [PF11].

## 4. Disagreements, boundaries, failures, negative space

Epsom salt's role is asserted without mechanism everywhere it appears in this pass: "spa
vibes" [PF8], an optional provider-suggested add-in [PF10] — no practitioner source here gave
it a specific physiological rationale, matching the same absence already noted on the
commercial web. Horse chestnut shows a direct internal tension: two sources describe its
venous-toning mechanism with evident enthusiasm [PF1] [PF4], but the same reference source
that supplies the mechanism also states it should be avoided in pregnancy and breastfeeding
[PF4] — and no pregnancy-focused source in this pass (the midwife-herbalist or either doula
practice) recommended oral horse chestnut at all. Its lineage here belongs entirely to
general-adult material, not the pregnancy/postpartum lens. No source reached in this pass
named warm-then-cold contrast bathing, repeating the same gap already found on the commercial
web. None of the pregnancy-specific sources distinguished a venous-pressure path from a
straining path; every pregnancy source treated constipation/straining as the assumed
mechanism, meaning the commission's proposed non-constipation venous-pressure account is
simply absent from the folk-web material reached here, not confirmed or denied. Failure
disclosure is uneven: the reader-remedy column explicitly reports remedies that did not work
and states when no evidence exists [PF9]; the Ayurvedic Q&A and both doula sources present
their recommendations with no acknowledgment of non-response [PF6] [PF7] [PF8].

## 5. Independence and commercial-entanglement notes

The herbalism-education blog discloses affiliate links and sells its own courses [PF3]; the
astringent-formula school's core business is paid herbalism certification, though the formula
article itself names no product to buy [PF1]. The midwife-herbalist's site monetizes through
her own courses and programs, but neither page fetched here pushed a named product [PF2]
[PF11]. The Ayurvedic Q&A responses recommend a specific branded product, Pilex (Himalaya),
inside otherwise free practitioner advice, with no vendor relationship disclosed [PF6] — a
soft, undisclosed commercial nudge worth flagging on its own terms. The doula-practice blogs
function as content marketing for booking doula services rather than for any product [PF7]
[PF8]. Christopher Hobbs' reference database, the 2008 personal blog, the nonprofit
postpartum educator, and the reader-remedy column showed the least visible commercial
entanglement of the sources reached in this pass [PF4] [PF5] [PF9] [PF10].

## 6. Access failures and unanswered questions

Susun Weed's susunweed.com returned a TLS certificate mismatch on direct fetch, and the
Wayback Machine was not reachable from this pass's tools; the well-known Wise Woman-tradition
view on pregnancy hemorrhoids could therefore not be verified directly and is omitted rather
than reported from search snippets alone. Appalachian folk-medicine archives were searched by
name for hemorrhoid/piles content; the one source opened directly named pine-bark
preparations for general complaints but did not mention hemorrhoids, so no
Appalachian-specific citation could be included without inventing detail. No independently
accessible podcast transcript specific to hemorrhoids was found beyond a podcast episode that
appears to mirror the astringent-formula blog post already cited [PF1]; it was not opened
separately since the written version was verified directly. Unanswered after this pass:
whether any folk or practitioner lineage gives warm-then-cold contrast bathing a rationale, and
whether any pregnancy-focused practitioner source anywhere distinguishes a venous-pressure
mechanism from straining.
