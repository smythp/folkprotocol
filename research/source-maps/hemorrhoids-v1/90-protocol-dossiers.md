# 90 — Protocol dossiers: hemorrhoids in pregnancy and postpartum

## Reconstruction boundary

These are the six directions selected in the assessment. A protocol is reconstructed only when a source actually supplies its preparation, route, amount or timing, and intended outcome. Product directions describe labeled use, not demonstrated comparative efficacy. Pregnancy and breastfeeding statements are attached to the particular preparation that supplied them; they do not transfer to another ingredient or formulation.

## 1. Non-constipation pregnancy pressure and positional offloading

### What the direction contains

The maps found three materially different actions under “position”: lying on the side, standing or moving after sitting, and reducing toilet sitting. A pregnancy thread described side-lying as feeling “a little better,” but only after a sitz bath, manual reduction, and cream, so neither the position nor its duration can be isolated [10:RF1]. Separate pregnancy-community and doula sources repeat avoiding prolonged sitting and lying on the side [50:EX10][60:PF7][60:PF8]. Feet-raised rest was not specified in any mapped source, and a toileting footstool has a worsening report [10:RF8].

The strongest directly sourced practice is therefore modest and nonspecific: interrupt prolonged chair sitting with movement or standing. ACOG says prolonged sitting puts pressure on pelvic veins and recommends getting up and moving; it also says pregnancy hemorrhoids commonly worsen just after delivery and then improve during the postpartum period [P1]. Left-side lying is a recurring account, but the directly opened forum excerpt gives no dose, onset, symptom type, or unconfounded result [P4]. No faithful feet-elevation protocol emerged.

### Causal account and disagreements

A clinical review attributes pregnancy hemorrhoids to several coexisting pressures: increased Valsalva during defecation, roughly 30% more blood volume, progesterone-related venous smooth-muscle relaxation, and uterine growth raising intra-abdominal pressure [P3]. That is the review authors’ explanation. It makes a non-constipation route plausible; it does not show that side-lying decompresses hemorrhoidal vessels or changes a hemorrhoid over days.

The prospective cohort followed 280 women to one month postpartum. Perianal disease appeared mainly in the third trimester and after delivery; constipation, more than 20 minutes of delivery straining, and birthweight above 3,800 g were independent predictors [P2]. This supports mechanical and bowel contributors without proving constipation is necessary. It also does not test ordinary chair position, side-lying, or feet elevation.

### Concrete boundaries and ordinary-use relevance

“Position” should not be presented as one intervention. Standing or moving addresses prolonged chair pressure; limiting toilet sitting addresses anorectal loading during defecation; side-lying is a comfort practice with no recovered schedule. A noticeable ordinary-use result would be less pressure or pain while in the changed position and return of discomfort when prolonged sitting resumes. No direct source establishes shrinkage, shorter flare duration, or recurrence prevention. The missing facts preventing a real side-lying protocol are duration, frequency, pregnancy stage, external versus internal disease, and an outcome observed without a bath, manual reduction, or topical treatment.

## 2. Iron form and schedule when constipation enters the chain

### What the direction contains

The mapped practice consists of moving an iron dose, switching to a chelated form, or taking iron on alternate days [10:RF4][40:CW10][50:EX9][60:PF11]. Only the first account links iron, constipation, and a new hemorrhoid; the maps found no hemorrhoid study of iron exposure (30-published-research §5).

Direct evidence supports a narrower proposition: form and elemental amount can change gastrointestinal tolerance in pregnancy. In a double-blind trial, 80 healthy pregnant women began at 15–19 weeks and took either 25 mg elemental iron as ferrous bisglycinate or 50 mg as ferrous sulfate daily until delivery. Iron-deficiency outcomes were similar, while gastrointestinal complaints were less frequent with bisglycinate [P6]. A later report combining two randomized pregnancy studies found that 80 mg/day ferrous fumarate was associated with more constipation and laxative use, while 20–60 mg/day was not dose-associated with gastrointestinal complaints; bisglycinate 25 mg/day had the most favorable gastrointestinal profile against fumarate 40 mg/day and sulfate 50 mg/day [P7]. Neither study measured hemorrhoids.

### Schedule, causation, and boundaries

WHO’s population guidance recommends 30–60 mg elemental iron plus 400 micrograms folic acid daily in pregnancy; it is intended to prevent maternal anemia and adverse birth outcomes, not manage hemorrhoids [P5]. That makes the elemental amount and reason for supplementation indispensable. “Chelated iron” without an elemental dose is not a reproducible specification, and a lower-dose bisglycinate trial cannot establish equivalence for a person being treated for diagnosed anemia.

The plausible operative chain is oral iron formulation or amount → bowel change → straining or harder stool → symptom aggravation. This is a mechanistic interpretation of the tolerance data and map account, not a demonstrated direct action of iron on hemorrhoidal tissue. Alternate-day dosing remains unresolved here: the mapped community used absorption logic [50:EX9], but no direct pregnancy source retrieved in this pass tied an alternate-day schedule to hemorrhoid or constipation outcomes.

### Ordinary-use relevance

No faithful hemorrhoid protocol emerges. In ordinary use, a meaningful signal from a clinically appropriate form or schedule change would first be easier stool passage or less constipation while iron status remains adequately treated; reduction in hemorrhoid pain or swelling would be downstream and was not measured. The missing facts are the indication for iron, baseline laboratory status, formulation and elemental amount, actual stool change, and whether one supervised change preserves correction or prevention of iron deficiency. Timing coincidence alone cannot separate iron from third-trimester pressure.

## 3. Sitz bathing: water parameters, Epsom salt, and contrast

### Plain warm water: the recoverable protocol

The mapped field treated warm bathing as a central practice while disagreeing about additives [10:RF2][10:RF6][40:CW5][40:CW6][50:EX7]. The clearest direct protocol uses a toilet-seat basin or shallow tub with enough water to cover the perineum, about 3–4 inches in a tub; average water temperature 104°F/40°C; and 15–20 minutes of soaking. Cleveland Clinic says plain warm water is sufficient, advises patting dry, and says a clinician may suggest three or four sessions daily when they provide relief [P8]. For pregnancy or recent birth, that page directs the reader to obtain timing instructions from the treating clinician [P8].

A physiological study exposed 18 healthy volunteers and 28 people with painful anorectal disease, including 10 with hemorrhoids, to 40°C, 45°C, and 50°C baths for 10 minutes. Anal pressure and internal-sphincter electrical activity fell during bathing and returned toward baseline over 25–70 minutes; pain relief lasted longer at higher temperatures. The authors proposed a neural “thermosphincteric” reflex [P9]. This supports transient sphincter relaxation as the study’s own explanation. It does not establish that hotter water is safer or heals hemorrhoids; the practical source caps the average at 40°C because hotter water can burn [P8].

### Epsom salt and contrast

No direct source supplied an Epsom concentration with a plain-water comparator. Cleveland Clinic warns that salts, oils, and other additives may inflame tissue [P8]. Community accounts range from pleasant or momentary relief to irritation and no healing [10:RF6][50:EX7]. A topical magnesium-salt soak also has no documented route for the commercial claim that it relieves constipation [40:CW5]. A warm-then-cold sequence did not appear in any of the six maps, and targeted searching found only a small general-adult trial comparing separate cold (<15°C) and warm (>30°C) baths. Among 24 randomized patients with hemorrhoids or fissures, it found no statistically significant pain difference over seven days [P10]. It did not test contrast.

### Ordinary-use relevance

For plain warm water, a noticeable result is reduced pain, itching, spasm, or irritation during the soak and for part of the following hour; durable shrinkage or healing over days is not established [P8][P9]. Epsom salt has no faithful dose, and contrast has no faithful sequence. Skin burning, increased irritation, or discomfort from the toilet-seat setup changes the usefulness of the practice; pregnancy or recent birth also changes when bathing is appropriate [P8].

## 4. Cold and witch-hazel contact across pads, padsicles, foam, and ice

### Separate preparations

The maps combine several products that need separation: room-temperature witch-hazel pads, refrigerated 50% foam, a frozen maxi pad containing witch hazel and optional aloe or oils, and a plain instant cold pack [10:RF5][40:CW2][40:CW3][40:CW9]. Cooling and astringency cannot be attributed to one another when both arrive on the same pad.

The reproducible labeled witch-hazel preparation is an external pad containing 50% w/w witch hazel. The label targets temporary itching, burning, irritation, and discomfort; it permits application up to six times daily or after bowel movements, or use as a moist compress for up to 15 minutes. It says not to insert the pad into the rectum and to stop if bleeding occurs or the condition fails to improve within seven days [P11]. These are label claims and limits, not trial outcomes.

The directly opened padsicle account is materially different. It puts 3–4 tablespoons of witch hazel down the middle of an extra-large absorbent pad, optionally adds aloe and oils, reseals and freezes it, thaws it for one or two minutes, and suggests another absorbent pad as a barrier if it feels too cold [P12]. The author, a labor-and-delivery nurse writing a commercial-affiliate blog, claims cooling, numbing, and swelling relief but supplies no hemorrhoid-specific follow-up or fixed contact time [P12]. Because the recipe can include aloe, lavender, coconut, or tea-tree oil, a favorable or irritating result cannot be assigned to witch hazel alone.

### What cold itself can and cannot supply

ACOG separately suggests an ice pack or witch-hazel pads for pregnancy hemorrhoids, which confirms that these are distinct practices [P1]. The closest controlled cold literature concerns postpartum perineal trauma rather than hemorrhoids: a Cochrane review found that ice or gel pads were generally applied for 10–20 minutes during the first two days after birth. Evidence for pain relief was low or very low certainty, and ice packs were not clearly better than cold gel pads [P13]. This supports a bounded cold-contact duration for injured postpartum perineal tissue, but cannot establish hemorrhoid shrinkage or the value of witch hazel.

### Ordinary-use relevance

A usable room-temperature witch-hazel-pad practice exists exactly as labeled; a noticeable result would be temporary reduction in external itching, burning, or discomfort during or soon after contact [P11]. A DIY frozen padsicle is reconstructable as a household recipe, but not as an attributable hemorrhoid protocol. Its ordinary signal is immediate cooling or numbing. The missing facts for more than comfort are hemorrhoid-specific contact time, use on intact versus torn or stitched postpartum tissue, a plain-cold comparator, and follow-up over days. Stinging, irritation, excessive cold, or contact with internal tissue are concrete reasons the formats diverge [10:RF7][P11][P12].

## 5. OTC topical actives by ingredient and reproductive stage

### Ingredient-level specifications

The maps correctly found that brand ladders conceal different jobs [10:RF9][40:CW1]. Current US labels make the distinctions concrete:

- Hydrocortisone 1% cream is an external anti-itch product, applied no more than three to four times daily. Its label says not to insert it and to stop if symptoms persist beyond seven days, recur shortly afterward, worsen, or bleed [P14]. It does not label itself as a shrinkage or anesthetic treatment.
- Phenylephrine HCl 0.25% ointment is a vasoconstrictor product labeled to temporarily shrink hemorrhoidal tissue and relieve burning. It may be used externally or intrarectally with its applicator up to four times daily, especially morning, night, and after bowel movements; the label sets a seven-day nonresponse boundary and asks pregnant or breastfeeding users to consult a professional [P15].
- Lidocaine 5% cream is an external local anesthetic for temporary pain, burning, itching, and discomfort, applied up to six times daily. It is not to be inserted, and its label also uses a seven-day nonresponse boundary [P16].
- Pramoxine HCl 1% plus zinc oxide 12.5% ointment combines a local anesthetic with a protectant. It is labeled for external itching, burning, and discomfort up to four times daily, especially morning, night, and after bowel movements, with a seven-day boundary and a pregnancy/breastfeeding consultation statement [P17].
- Witch hazel 50% is the external astringent pad preparation described in dossier 4, used up to six times daily or after bowel movements [P11].

These amounts and schedules belong to the cited finished formulations. Combination products cannot be treated as evidence for one active, and external directions cannot be silently converted into intrarectal use.

### Pregnancy, postpartum, and breastfeeding

UKTIS reports that topical hemorrhoid products can contain astringents, local anesthetics, corticosteroids, and other ingredients in combinations; none are licensed specifically for pregnancy. Routine short use is based on low expected fetal exposure, while pregnancy-outcome data remain limited [P18]. This is more restrained than either a blanket “unsafe” or a blanket “pregnancy safe” claim. During breastfeeding, NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service considers topical or rectal lidocaine, hydrocortisone or prednisolone, and zinc oxide compatible because the dose, area, and systemic absorption are small; it advises the lowest effective exposure for the shortest duration and handwashing before touching the infant [P19].

The mapped claim that prolonged steroid use thins perianal skin came from forum discussion [10:RF9]. The direct hydrocortisone label does not name thinning; its concrete boundary is seven days without clinician review [P14]. No opened trial supplied comparative pregnancy outcomes for these individual OTC actives.

### Ordinary-use relevance

The expected noticeable result follows the active: less itch with hydrocortisone, temporary numbness or less pain with lidocaine or pramoxine, temporary reduction in swollen tissue or burning with phenylephrine, and temporary relief of external itch or burning with witch hazel [P11][P14][P15][P16][P17]. No label establishes recurrence prevention. “Plateau” is therefore not a stronger-brand threshold: bleeding, worsening, applicator pain, or persistence past the product’s seven-day boundary are the actual label limits.

## 6. Oral venotonics without compound flattening

### MPFF and diosmin

MPFF is a defined micronized purified flavonoid fraction, not a synonym for any diosmin supplement. Daflon 500 contains 450 mg diosmin and 50 mg flavonoids expressed as hesperidin per tablet. Its product information gives an acute hemorrhoidal-attack schedule of six tablets daily for four days, then four daily for three days, by mouth with meals. The same document prefers avoiding it during pregnancy because human data are absent or limited and says infant risk during breastfeeding cannot be excluded [P21].

The pregnancy report involved 50 women with acute internal hemorrhoids taking micronized diosmin 90%/hesperidin 10% for a median eight weeks before delivery and four weeks afterward. It was open-label and uncontrolled; 66% had relief from acute symptoms by day four, and the authors reported no detected effects on pregnancy, fetal development, birthweight, infant growth, or feeding [P20]. The abstract does not expose the full dosing sequence, so the commercial seven-day schedule must not be substituted for the study protocol. Unmicronized diosmin alone had no pregnancy protocol or separable outcome in the sources opened here.

### Hydroxyethylrutosides

A double-blind placebo-controlled trial randomized 97 pregnant women with first- to third-degree hemorrhoids to oral O-(beta-hydroxyethyl)rutosides 500 mg twice daily or placebo. Patient-rated pain, bleeding, exudation, and pruritus and clinician-rated signs improved more with rutosides after two and four weeks; three mild transient side effects occurred in the active group [P22]. A pregnancy Cochrane review pooled two rutoside trials totaling 150 women and found fewer nonresponders, but concluded safety evidence was insufficient for a recommendation; one congenital malformation occurred in an exposed pregnancy and one fetal death in a control pregnancy, without an established causal link [P23]. This is a real pregnancy efficacy signal with an unresolved reproductive-safety record, not evidence transferable to rutin or MPFF.

### Euphorbia prostrata

The pregnancy study used branded Sitcom: one oral tablet containing 100 mg Euphorbia prostrata once daily plus a 1% w/w topical cream for 14 days. It was a prospective, multicenter, single-arm study of 100 pregnant women, so oral and topical contributions cannot be separated. Mean bleeding and defecation-pain scores fell 86.6% and 49.3% at two weeks; by eight weeks, reported reductions were 77.6% for defecation pain, 96.9% for itching, 100% for exudate, and 84.5% for swelling. The investigators reported no maternal or newborn adverse events [P24]. The paper attributes possible activity to flavonoids, phenolic acids, and tannins with anti-inflammatory, analgesic, hemostatic, astringent, and vasoprotective actions; those are mechanistic interpretations, and the uncontrolled dual-route design cannot establish which action produced the result [P24].

### Horse chestnut and butcher’s broom

Horse chestnut must specify seed extract. The EMA monograph addresses Aesculus hippocastanum seed preparations, with established use for chronic venous insufficiency rather than hemorrhoids. Its standardized oral dry extract supplies 21 mg triterpene glycosides calculated as protoaescigenin twice daily, and benefit may require at least four weeks. Pregnancy and lactation safety are not established, so use is not recommended; reported adverse effects include gastrointestinal complaints, headache, vertigo, itching, and allergic reactions [P25]. The hemorrhoid enthusiasm and self-built 1,000-mg stacks in the maps [10:RF7][50:EX5] do not state the extract ratio or aescin standardization and are not equivalent to this preparation.

Butcher’s broom is Ruscus aculeatus rhizome, not horse chestnut. EMA recognizes only traditional use for hemorrhoid-associated itching and burning after serious causes are excluded. The monograph permits, among other distinct preparations, powdered rhizome 350 mg three times daily or aqueous dry extract (DER 2.5–6.5:1) 150–200 mg per dose to a maximum 450 mg/day, with review if symptoms persist beyond two weeks. Pregnancy and lactation safety are unestablished, so use is not recommended [P26]. The map’s Mediterranean, Balkan, and Levantine record supports lineage, not a pregnancy claim [20:TR10].

### Cross-compound interpretation and ordinary-use relevance

Across 24 randomized phlebotonic studies, a Cochrane review found pooled benefit for itching, bleeding, discharge or leakage, and overall symptoms, but no statistically significant pain benefit. It describes the proposed class actions as improved venous tone, stabilized capillary permeability, and increased lymphatic drainage while saying the precise mechanism is not established [P27]. Heterogeneous compounds and mostly nonpregnant populations prevent that pooled result from supplying a pregnancy dose for any one product.

Usable study protocols exist for rutosides and for the inseparable oral-plus-topical Euphorbia regimen; MPFF has a defined product schedule and a separate uncontrolled pregnancy record whose complete dose was not retrievable. An ordinary noticeable result, where reported, is symptom relief by day four for MPFF, change in pain/bleeding/itch/exudation by two to four weeks for rutosides, or multi-symptom change after the two-week Euphorbia course [P20][P22][P24]. No faithful pregnancy or breastfeeding trial emerges for horse chestnut, butcher’s broom, unmicronized diosmin, or self-built multi-compound stacks.

## Research spillover

The targeted search found controlled pregnancy evidence for hydroxyethylrutosides that was underdeveloped in the maps; it belongs inside the selected venotonic direction rather than as a new dossier [P22][P23]. A postpartum cold literature exists for perineal trauma, but its tissue target and outcomes do not establish a frozen-witch-hazel hemorrhoid protocol [P13]. No direct protocol emerged for warm–cold contrast, feet elevation, or changing iron solely for hemorrhoid relief.
