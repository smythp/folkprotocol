# 40 — Commercial Web Map: Hemorrhoids, Pregnancy and Postpartum

Commission: hemorrhoids-v1. Scope: adults managing hemorrhoids at home, pregnancy/postpartum
as primary lens. Map of the commercial web only — what is sold, how it is described, who sells
it. Not a verdict on efficacy. Access date for all sources unless noted: 2026-09-21.

## 1. Search routes and market segments visited

Routes: named-product queries (Preparation H, Tucks, Frida Mom, Motherlove, Daflon, Venastat),
ingredient queries (phenylephrine, pramoxine/lidocaine, hydrocortisone, witch hazel, diosmin,
horse chestnut), format queries (sitz bath basin, Epsom salt soak, padsicle, ring cushion),
pregnancy-safety queries per ingredient, market-sizing queries, affiliate/ownership queries, and
a patent-vocabulary query. Amazon customer reviews are JS-rendered and did not return through
the fetch tool available in this pass (§6); product title/description text did return.

Segments encountered: drugstore OTC topicals (Preparation H [CW1], Tucks [CW2]); DTC/lifestyle
postpartum-care brand (Frida [CW3]); small independent herbal brand (Motherlove [CW4]);
DTC supplement seller (Doctor Butler's [CW5]); hospital-system patient education (Cleveland
Clinic [CW6]); business-opportunity media aimed at sellers, not patients (capitalism.com [CW7]);
affiliate content site that also sells its own branded line (hemorrhoid.com/Revivol-XR [CW8]);
independent parenting/birth-worker blogger (mommylabornurse.com [CW9]); DTC prenatal-vitamin
brand (Needed [CW10]); international branded pharmaceutical (Daflon/Servier [CW11]); and generic
Amazon-marketplace durable good (ring cushion [CW12]).

## 2. Product and claim vocabulary

- "Multi-symptom relief" / "instant relief from pain, burning, itching and swelling" — Preparation
  H's framing for its lidocaine+phenylephrine line, echoed loosely across the category [CW1].
- "Cools on contact" / "soothing medication that cools" — recurs for witch hazel formats
  specifically (Preparation H wipes for women [CW1], Frida foam [CW3]).
- "Gentle," "medicated," "hospital-used" — Tucks leans on clinical-adjacent language without
  claiming drug status beyond its witch hazel monograph [CW2].
- "Stops hemorrhoids at the source" — Doctor Butler's Epsom salt copy, implying a systemic
  (constipation-relieving) effect from a topical soak [CW5].
- "Organic," "steroid-free," "no petroleum/parabens/phthalates" — Motherlove's clean-label
  vocabulary, contrasted implicitly against the OTC drug category [CW4].
- "Non-constipating," "gentle on stomach," "clinically shown to cause fewer GI upsets" — the
  chelated-iron pitch, aimed directly at avoiding the straining pathway [CW10].
- "PT ENDORSED" — a credential-flavored brand name on a ring cushion with no named practitioner
  or study attached [CW12].
- "8-figure exit," "steal this business" — capitalism.com's register is seller-facing, not
  patient-facing, describing the category as investable rather than describing symptoms [CW7].

## 3. Recurring formulation and narrative families

**Family A — Witch hazel contact/cooling products.** The single most repeated active across
segments: Tucks pads (50% witch hazel solution) [CW2], Frida's foam (50% witch hazel, refrigerable)
[CW3], Preparation H's witch hazel wipe lines [CW1], and the DIY "padsicle" (witch hazel-soaked
pad, frozen, sometimes with aloe/lavender) promoted by birth-worker bloggers [CW9]. The shared
story is astringent/cooling contact relief, explicitly for both hemorrhoidal and general perineal
tissue, and explicitly marketed to the same pregnancy-to-postpartum customer across all four
sources. Padsicles are the DIY/no-brand version of the same formulation logic as the branded pads.

**Family B — Warm soak, with a live disagreement about additives.** Doctor Butler's Epsom Salt
Soak claims Epsom salt "reduces inflammation and helps stop hemorrhoids at the source by
relieving constipation," sold as an add-on to warm water [CW5]. Cleveland Clinic's patient page
gives 104°F/15–20 minutes as the protocol and states plainly: "Warm water alone works. Salts,
oils and other substances may cause inflammation" [CW6]. Same practice, opposite claim about the
same additive, from a seller versus a hospital-system source — see §4.

**Family C — Topical OTC drug actives, tiered by mechanism.** Preparation H's own product-line
split names three distinct actives sold in parallel: pramoxine alone (topical anesthetic),
lidocaine + phenylephrine (anesthetic + vasoconstrictor, framed as "rapid"/"instant"), and witch
hazel (astringent, framed as gentlest and the only line explicitly marketed for pregnancy) [CW1].
Tucks occupies the witch hazel tier only [CW2]. This maps directly onto the commission's
active-ingredient axis: the vasoconstrictor and anesthetic actives are marketed as stronger but
are not the ones pointed at pregnant customers by name.

**Family D — Clean-label herbal salve as the pregnancy-safe alternative.** Motherlove's balm
(olive oil, beeswax, witch hazel, plantain, yarrow, calendula) is positioned explicitly against
the steroid/drug category — "steroid-free... great for daily use" — for exactly the pregnancy and
postpartum window where OTC drug labels hedge [CW4]. This is a distinct narrative from Family A:
not "cooling contact relief" but "safe for continuous use because it isn't a drug."

**Family E — Oral venotonics, two markets apart.** Daflon (diosmin 90%/hesperidin 10%, MPFF) is
sold as a branded pharmaceutical outside the US with an explicit acute-flare dosing schedule (6
tablets/day for 4 days, tapering) [CW11]. No equivalent branded drug reached in the US market;
instead the domestic shelf holds diosmin/hesperidin combined with horse chestnut in unbranded
supplement stacks (noted via search, not opened directly — snippet-only, §7). The two markets
sell the same active-ingredient logic under different regulatory dress.

**Family F — Positional/durable goods and the iron-timing adjacent play.** Ring/donut cushions
are sold as a single SKU across unrelated causes — hemorrhoids, coccyx pain, "prostate,"
postpartum, pressure sores — with no cause-specific claim differentiating pregnancy from any
other seated-pain use [CW12]. Separately, chelated prenatal iron brands market their form and
dose explicitly against the constipation pathway to hemorrhoids, without naming hemorrhoids
directly [CW10] — an adjacent-market response to the same commission lead about iron timing/form.

## 4. Disagreements, customer-reported failures, and negative space

- Epsom salt in a sitz bath: seller claims added benefit including a systemic
  constipation effect [CW5]; hospital patient education says plain warm water works and additives
  "may cause inflammation" [CW6]. Neither source engages the other directly.
- Pregnancy status for the same active ingredient varies by brand copy rather than by ingredient:
  Preparation H markets one witch hazel product by name for pregnancy [CW1] while its
  phenylephrine/lidocaine lines carry no pregnancy-specific claim either way in the copy fetched.
- Drug-category framing is inconsistent: Motherlove's core sell is explicitly not being a steroid
  [CW4], while Cleveland Clinic's protocol advice (temperature, duration) does not engage
  botanical alternatives at all [CW6] — the clinic and the herbal seller do not appear to be
  arguing with each other so much as occupying separate literatures.
- No source in this pass gave a mechanism for why "warm-then-cold contrast" (named in the
  commission seeds) would outperform either alone; none of the twelve pages mentioned contrast
  bathing at all — a gap, not a finding.
- Customer-review sentiment (failure reports, "didn't work," return complaints) was not
  recoverable: Amazon serves reviews via client-side JavaScript the fetch tool in this pass could
  not execute, so vendor claims in §2–3 are unbalanced by visible customer pushback (§6).
- The ring cushion's marketing makes no distinction between a pregnant user's shifting anatomy and
  a general "sitting on a sore tailbone" user [CW12] — the commission's positional-offloading lead
  is not met by any product-level differentiation found.

## 5. Copying, ownership, affiliate, and incentive notes

hemorrhoid.com publishes "guide" content that names Tucks by product but concludes by steering
readers to Revivol-XR, a line sold by the same site, with no affiliate/sponsorship disclosure
visible on the page fetched — an undisclosed self-referral rather than independent comparison
[CW8]. capitalism.com's piece is written for people considering entering the hemorrhoid-product
business, not for patients, and names Doctor Butler's, Frida, Preparation H, and Tucks together
as the incumbent brand set worth displacing — useful as an outside confirmation of which brands
dominate shelf space, but written with a sales incentive of its own (selling a business-acquisition
pitch) [CW7]. Motherlove and Doctor Butler's are both small independent DTC sellers whose
product-page copy is original rather than templated; Preparation H, Tucks, and Frida are each a
single larger-brand product line with consistent house copy across their own SKUs. No verbatim
copy block was found repeated across unrelated owners in this pass — the cross-vendor overlap is
in ingredient choice and claim structure (Family A), not in shared sentences.

## 6. Access failures and unanswered questions

Amazon customer reviews did not render through the available fetch tool (JS-dependent); one
product page (a second ring-cushion listing) returned a server error and was replaced with a
working listing from a different seller [CW12]. Trustpilot-style review aggregation for any named
hemorrhoid product was not attempted directly in this pass. The US retail landscape for
diosmin/horse chestnut combination supplements was seen only in search-result summary form, not
opened directly (snippet-only, no ID assigned — excluded per the one-ID-per-page rule since no
single stable product URL was verified). Open questions the commercial web did not answer:
whether any seller states a mechanism for warm-then-cold contrast; whether any topical product
distinguishes the venous-pressure path from the straining path named in the commission seeds
(none of the twelve sources drew this distinction); and whether frozen-witch-hazel products carry
any frostbite/tissue-cold caution comparable to the instant-cold perineal pads named in the
commission's home-kit seed.
