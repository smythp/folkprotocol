# Published Research Map — Hemorrhoids, Pregnancy, and Postpartum

Commission: practices reducing pain, swelling, itch, and recurrence of
hemorrhoids, and what changes in pregnancy/postpartum. See
`00-commission.md` for full boundary.

## 1. Indexes and search routes used

General web search (built-in tool) as primary discovery route, querying
PubMed-indexed terminology, specific compounds (diosmin, aescin,
phenylephrine), and named research groups; surfaced PubMed, PMC, Cochrane
Library, Frontiers, ScienceDirect, and Cureus listings.
Direct WebFetch of `pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` was blocked by a cookie-consent
wall every time; citation and abstract data instead came from the Europe PMC
REST API (`ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:<PMID>`).
PMC full-text pages rendered directly for open-access articles. No Chinese-,
Korean-, Japanese-, or Sanskrit-language database was searched natively;
non-Western material found came through English-language indexing (Frontiers,
Cureus, Ayu). One search-engine summary claimed a 2023 Cochrane review of
hydrocortisone for hemorrhoids by "Albuquerque et al."; a direct Europe PMC
search for that author/topic returned no such record, so this is treated as
a confabulation, not a source. Search stopped at 12 when further queries
returned the same research groups and preparations, not new programs.

## 2. Vocabulary and research terrain

Anal cushions are treated as normal structures present from birth,
maintaining 15-20% of resting anal pressure and aiding continence
discrimination between gas, liquid, and solid; four recurring mechanistic
concepts recur across reviews — sliding/loss of the cushions'
connective-tissue fixation, vascular dysregulation (arterial inflow, venous
hypertension, neovascularization), rectal redundancy, and elevated anorectal
pressure [PR6][PR8]. "Phlebotonics" (venotonics) is the umbrella term for a
heterogeneous drug class — plant flavonoids (diosmin, hesperidin, rutosides)
and synthetic agents (calcium dobesilate) — claimed to improve venous tone,
capillary permeability, and lymphatic drainage without a fully established
mechanism [PR6]. "Micronized purified flavonoid fraction" (MPFF, branded
Daflon) denotes a specific 90% diosmin/10% hesperidin preparation,
distinguished from unmicronized diosmin [PR4]. Internal hemorrhoid grading
(I-IV by prolapse) is presupposed by trial inclusion criteria rather than
explained. "Arsha" is the Ayurvedic disease category loosely mapped onto
hemorrhoids, with "Kshara" (alkaline cauterant preparations) as a named
traditional intervention class distinct from home practice [PR12
background]. No source used a term distinguishing a "venous-pressure"
mechanism from a "straining" mechanism as named, competing models; that
distinction is this commission's framing, not the field's (researcher
inference).

## 3. Major research programs or strains of thought

**Pregnancy-specific epidemiology from one Lithuanian research group.** A
prospective cohort of 280 pregnant women followed to one month postpartum
found 43.9% developed perianal disease, concentrated in the third trimester
(61% of cases) and postpartum (34.1%); personal history of perianal disease,
constipation, straining during delivery over 20 minutes, and newborn
birthweight over 3800g were independent predictors [PR2]. The same group
later ran an RCT (n=260) of structured dietary/behavioral counseling (fluid
targets, fiber, exercise, defecation technique) begun before 12 weeks'
gestation, finding a large reduction in postpartum hemorrhoid rate
(intention-to-treat RR 0.38) [PR3]. Together these model hemorrhoid risk
through modifiable straining behavior and non-modifiable mechanical load
(birthweight, delivery time) — a straining-centered account — without
measuring a separate venous-pressure pathway (relaxed vein walls, expanded
blood volume, uterine compression) as a distinct variable.

**Global descriptive epidemiology.** A 2025/2026 systematic review and
meta-analysis pooling 150 studies (~8.96 million people) put global point
prevalence at 25.92% (95% CI 22.62-29.22), rising to 31.01% among pregnant
women specifically, with pregnancy an independent risk factor (OR 2.61)
behind family history (OR 4.19) [PR1].

**Open-label, single-arm pregnancy drug trials.** Two structurally similar
studies test an oral-plus-topical botanical/flavonoid product in pregnant
women with existing hemorrhoids, neither randomized nor placebo-controlled.
Buckshee et al. (1997) gave micronized diosmin 90%/hesperidin 10% to 50
pregnant outpatients on a tapering schedule from 8 weeks before to 4 weeks
after delivery, reporting 66% symptom relief by day 4 and no apparent
effect on fetal development or feeding [PR4]. Porwal et al. (2024) gave 100
pregnant women oral Euphorbia prostrata 100mg plus 1% topical cream for 14
days, reporting up to 100% relief on some domains by 8 weeks and no adverse
events [PR5]. Both measure "safe and well tolerated" through absence of
reported adverse events in an unblinded, uncontrolled design, not a
comparison arm.

**Oral phlebotonic and horse chestnut evidence outside pregnancy.** A
Cochrane review of phlebotonics for hemorrhoids (24 RCTs, mostly
non-pregnant adults) found significant benefit for pruritus, bleeding,
discharge, and overall symptom improvement, but not pain [PR6]. A separate
Cochrane review of oral horse chestnut seed extract (HCSE) for chronic
venous insufficiency generally (not hemorrhoid-specific) found reduced leg
pain and volume versus placebo, with authors calling larger confirmatory
trials still needed [PR7]. Neither review's trials enrolled pregnant
participants.

**Traditional and herbal medicine reviewed, not tested, in aggregate.** A
narrative review synthesizes proposed mechanisms and cited (not
independently re-verified here) trial evidence for witch hazel, horse
chestnut, and polyherbal Ayurvedic formulations (Triphala Guggulu, Pilex),
describing anti-inflammatory, venotonic, astringent, and wound-healing
effects, while flagging standardization and clinical validation as
unresolved [PR8]. A separate review surveys proposed mechanisms of
Traditional Chinese Medicine preparations specifically, motivated by
surgical cost and complications, stating plainly that summary literature on
TCM's mechanism is scarce [PR12].

**Post-surgical, not home, sitz bath evidence.** The only sitz bath RCT
located studied post-hemorrhoidectomy patients, not general or
pregnancy-related hemorrhoids: 50 patients randomized to antibiotics/
analgesics alone versus the same plus twice-daily warm sitz bath, finding no
significant difference in pain score or analgesic use [PR9].

## 4. Null results, disputes, and neglected questions

- **Sitz bath: the one RCT located is a null result, drawn from the wrong
  population for this commission.** [PR9] found no significant pain or
  analgesic-use benefit from twice-daily warm sitz baths, but its 50
  patients were recovering from surgical hemorrhoidectomy, not managing
  hemorrhoids conservatively at home or in pregnancy — a mismatch limiting
  how far the null result generalizes (section 5). No RCT of temperature,
  duration, warm-then-cold contrast, or Epsom salt addition was located at
  all — an outright gap, not a null result.
- **No RCT evidence for oral flavonoid or Euphorbia therapy in pregnancy —
  only open-label, uncontrolled reports.** [PR4] and [PR5] report favorable
  outcomes, but neither has a control arm, so improvement cannot be
  separated from natural postpartum resolution (both extend follow-up to 30
  days postpartum, when prevalence is known to fall independent of
  treatment per [PR2]).
- **Phlebotonics help several symptoms but not pain, and were not studied
  in pregnancy in the pooled trials.** [PR6]'s null result specifically for
  pain (versus bleeding, itch, discharge) is a finding this commission's own
  framing (pain, swelling, itch, recurrence) should not flatten into one
  "works/doesn't work" verdict.
- **Horse chestnut's pregnancy safety is an acknowledged absence, not a
  contested finding.** [PR7]'s trials evaluate venous insufficiency
  generally; no source located reports a trial of HCSE in pregnant
  hemorrhoid patients or makes an affirmative pregnancy safety claim — an
  absence of data, not a cited primary safety study (researcher inference
  from the gap itself).
- **No current systematic review of topical OTC treatments (phenylephrine,
  hydrocortisone, pramoxine, witch hazel) exists.** The relevant Cochrane
  review was withdrawn by the Cochrane Colorectal group as failing current
  methodological standards, "no current plans to revise or republish"
  [PR11] — a structural gap in exactly the territory the commission is most
  interested in.
- **A published dispute exists over the global prevalence meta-analysis
  itself.** [PR1] has a subsequent comment/letter and author response in
  the same journal (identified by title, not retrieved in full — section
  6), indicating the pooled prevalence figures are not uncontested.

## 5. Material, preparation, population, or outcome mismatches

**Population mismatch dominates the sitz bath evidence base.** The only
sitz bath RCT located studies post-surgical patients [PR9], not the
conservative, non-surgical, pregnant/postpartum population this commission
centers — this pass cannot say whether sitz baths help ordinary at-home
symptom management, only that they show no signal in a wound-healing
context. **Preparation mismatch runs through the herbal literature**:
[PR8] discusses polyherbal Ayurvedic formulations (Triphala Guggulu, Pilex)
and named single plants without this pass locating a placebo-controlled
trial of the branded polyherbal products themselves, as opposed to
component review claims. **Outcome-measurement mismatch**: [PR4] and [PR5]
report "symptom relief" through investigator- or patient-rated ordinal
scores at study-specific timepoints with no shared instrument between them,
and neither uses a scale independently validated for pregnancy (versus the
general grading literature, which grades prolapse severity, not symptom
burden). **Dosing/schedule mismatch on iron**: no pregnancy hemorrhoid study
located measured or controlled for concurrent iron supplementation; [PR2]
and [PR3] do not report iron intake among analyzed factors, leaving the
commissioner's iron-timing lead unaddressed here (an absence in what this
search located, not confirmed silence in the wider literature).
**Guideline scope mismatch**: the ESCP guideline [PR10] aims at guidance on
the "most effective (surgical) treatment," with six recommendation sections
including "special situations," but this pass could not confirm from the
abstract whether pregnancy falls there or what conservative-care
recommendations say — an access limitation (section 6), not an assumption.

## 6. Access failures and unanswered questions

Direct WebFetch of PubMed pages failed every time (cookie-consent
interstitial); the Europe PMC REST API supplied verified metadata and
abstract text for every PMID below, recorded as abstract-only unless a PMC
full-text fetch is separately noted. WebFetch of the LWW-hosted 2024
ASCRS clinical practice guideline returned HTTP 402; it is not cited here
because its conservative-management content and evidence grades could not
be verified — only its existence (Hawkins et al., *Diseases of the Colon &
Rectum* 2024;67(5):614-623) confirmed via PubMed's plain-text record, which
had no structured abstract. The PDF of the ESCP guideline [PR10] returned
largely non-extractable text, so this map relies on its abstract only, not
its recommendation tables. A comment and author response disputing [PR1]
were identified by title (Wang L., comment; Ebrahimi P., response; both
*Annals of Medicine* 2026) but the comment returned HTTP 403 on fetch, so
its argument is not characterized beyond noting the dispute exists. No
primary study addressing iron form, dose, or schedule as a variable in
pregnancy hemorrhoid risk was located despite targeted search; iron-dosing
pregnancy RCTs exist (constipation as a general side effect) but none
cross-referenced hemorrhoid outcomes. No study of frozen witch hazel pads,
instant-cold perianal pads, or the "padsicle" as a distinct cold-application
practice was located in any index searched. No positional-intervention
trial (left side-lying, elevated feet, less toilet-sitting time) specific
to hemorrhoids was located; the left-lateral-position literature found
concerns venocaval compression in labor, not hemorrhoid symptoms, and is
not cited here as it would misrepresent that literature's subject.
