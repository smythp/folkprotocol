# Traditional Lineages — Hemorrhoids, Pregnancy, and Postpartum

Map of named traditions and inherited practice for: what reduces hemorrhoid
pain, swelling, itch, and recurrence, with pregnancy and postpartum as the
primary lens. Access date for every source is 2026-09-21 unless stated.

## 1. Traditions and search routes visited

Search passes run for: Ayurveda (Sanskrit classical texts and secondary
review literature on *Arshas*); Traditional Chinese Medicine (pattern
differentiation, external "fumigation-wash" therapy, named formulas, a
still-sold patent ointment); Unani/Greco-Arabic humoral medicine (*Bawasir*,
Avicenna's Canon); Talmudic/rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud, tractate
Avodah Zarah); Indigenous North American plant knowledge as it enters
Euro-American herbal use (witch hazel); and European Mediterranean/Balkan
folk botany (horse chestnut, butcher's broom). Existing Folkprotocol material
was not consulted as a starting point, per commission.

Searches for African, Latin American/Caribbean, Nordic, and Korean
hemorrhoid-specific lineages turned up only generic modern review articles
repeating the same herb list without regional attribution; these are treated
as negative space (§4) rather than forced into the map. Roughly 30 documents
were opened or attempted; 12 are used below, prioritizing sources with a
named tradition, a specific document, or a directly quoted/paraphrased
classical passage over generic "natural remedies" aggregation.

## 2. Native vocabulary and conceptual terrain

No tradition surveyed treats "hemorrhoids" as a single bounded entity the way
a modern OTC aisle does; each embeds it in a broader causal scheme.

**Ayurveda — *Arshas* (अर्श).** One of Sushruta's classical disease
categories, subdivided by which *dosha* (Vata, Pitta, Kapha) or combination
predominates, plus a *Raktaja* (blood-caused) and a *Sahaja* (congenital)
type — six named types in total, each with its own described appearance,
discharge, and pain quality [TR1][TR2].

**Chinese — 痔 (zhì).** Not one pattern but four distinct ones in the
source consulted — damp-heat, large-intestine heat, qi-and-blood stagnation,
and spleen-qi sinking — each with its own pulse/tongue signs, triggers, and
opposite bathing advice (see §4) [TR4].

**Unani/Greco-Arabic — *Bawasir* (بواسير).** Framed through humoral
pathology: an accumulation of "abnormal melancholic humour" (black bile,
*sawda*), with melancholic temperament as a predisposing constitution, not a
local vascular event [TR6].

**Talmudic — פיקעא (piqa).** A rectal-crevice affliction the Talmud
splits by anatomical site — "upper" versus "lower" crevice — each drawing a
different named remedy rather than one general treatment [TR8].

**Indigenous North American / Euro-American herbal — witch hazel.** No
single disease name surfaced in the sources opened; witch hazel is folded
into a broader astringent-bark category treating diarrhea, dysentery,
mucous discharge, and hemorrhoids together, on the logic of tannin
astringency rather than a named hemorrhoid category [TR9].

## 3. Major pathways or strains of thought

**Ayurveda.** Sushruta's Nidana Sthana names six Arshas types: Vataja (dry,
irregular, "kadamba-flower" or needle-like masses, with constipation and
lower-back/abdominal pain), Pittaja (thin, yellowish, "parrot-tongue"
masses discharging blood-mixed liquid stool, with fever), Kaphaja (white,
deep-rooted, immobile, with mucousy diarrhea and cold), Raktaja
(nyagrodha-sprout-like, sudden heavy bleeding), Sannipataja (all three
doshas), and Sahaja (congenital, poor prognosis) [TR1]. Cause is attributed
to incompatible food combinations, prolonged sitting, suppressing natural
urges, and excess sexual activity in the unrestrained [TR1]. Location
governs curability: external and middle rectal folds are treatable, deep
interior folds are not [TR1]. Treatment is staged rather than a single
remedy: *Bheshaja* (medicine and diet) first, escalating through *Kshara*
(caustic-paste application), *Agni Karma* (thermal cautery), to *Shastra*
(surgical excision) only if earlier stages fail [TR2]. Postpartum care
(*Sutika Paricharya*) treats the twelve weeks after placental expulsion as
its own regimen — tissue restoration, uterine blood management, and
digestive-fire support — as a period of general heightened vulnerability
rather than one naming hemorrhoids specifically [TR3].

**Traditional Chinese Medicine.** Zhì is differentiated into at least four
patterns, each with matched herbs and a matched bathing temperature (§4):
damp-heat in the lower burner (Si Miao San), large-intestine heat with
fresh bleeding (Liang Xue Di Huang Tang, cooling herbs Huai Hua and Di Yu),
qi-and-blood stagnation with dark, hard, prolapsed tissue (Tao Hong Si Wu
Tang), and spleen-qi sinking with bearing-down prolapse (Bu Zhong Yi Qi
Tang, moxibustion at Baihui to lift sinking qi) [TR4]. A still-manufactured
patent formula, Ma Ying Long ointment, applies the damp-heat/toxin model
directly to topical practice: borneol, amber, smithsonite, cattle
gallstone, musk, and pearl, combined to "clear heat, relieve toxicity" and
promote tissue healing [TR5].

**Unani/Greco-Arabic medicine.** Causation is humoral: thick, viscous
melancholic blood pools in the anal veins, a constitutional (melancholic
temperament) vulnerability rather than a local mechanical one [TR6].
Treatment principle is evacuation — concoctive and laxative therapy to
resolve the accumulated humor before addressing the local mass [TR6].
Avicenna's Canon (Book 3, chapter on anal disease) is reported, via
secondary literature not opened directly, to classify hemorrhoids as
internal or external, to favor conservative measures (laxatives, guggul,
linseed) first, to use leeching and venesection to reduce local blood
excess, and to reserve ligation and surgical cutting for advanced cases
[TR7, snippet-only].

**Talmudic/rabbinic tradition.** Avodah Zarah 28a–29a records site-specific
remedies attributed to named sages, offered as sequential alternatives
rather than a single formula: for the lower crevice, melted fat from a goat
that has not given birth, or ashes of shade-dried pumpkin leaves; elsewhere
in the passage, a bramble kernel applied "crevice against crevice," snail
shells, and an oil-and-wax dressing on worn cloth [TR8]. The text itself
frames these as things reported to have worked for specific individuals,
not a general prescription.

**Indigenous North American plant knowledge / Euro-American herbalism.**
Witch hazel (*Hamamelis virginiana*) was, per a secondary pharmacological
review, "well known to the native people of North America," who used a
bark decoction for its astringency against diarrhea, dysentery, excess
mucous discharge, and hemorrhoids, alongside topical use for skin
inflammation [TR9]. The source does not name a specific nation or cite a
primary ethnobotanical record, and does not cover the plant's transition
into 19th-century Euro-American commercial distillation — a gap, not a
finding (§5).

**European Mediterranean/Balkan folk botany.** Two distinct venotonic
plants, often run together in modern summaries (§5) but attested
separately here. Butcher's broom (*Ruscus aculeatus*) rhizome/root appears
by name in Palestinian, south Balkan, eastern Mediterranean, Turkish,
Greek, and central Italian folk medicine for venous insufficiency,
hemorrhoids, edema, and skin ailments — named region by region, not
pooled as "European folk medicine" [TR10]. Horse chestnut (*Aesculus
hippocastanum*) is native to the Balkan peninsula; a commercial secondary
source (not independently verified) dates the first record of its use
specifically for hemorrhoids to 1800, and names seed, bark, leaf, and
flower as the historically used parts [TR11].

## 4. Variations, disagreements, and negative space

**Warm is not universal in TCM.** Against the assumption that a warm sitz
bath is simply "the" traditional practice, the TCM source consulted
prescribes *cool* sitz baths for damp-heat and heat patterns and reserves
*warm* baths for qi-stagnation patterns — temperature is diagnostic, not a
fixed rule [TR4].

**Staged severity vs. flat OTC framing.** Ayurveda's Bheshaja→Kshara→Agni→
Shastra sequence explicitly escalates by failure of the prior stage [TR2];
nothing in Unani or Talmudic material surveyed carries an equivalent staged
structure — Unani favors evacuation throughout [TR6], and the Talmud offers
parallel alternatives, not a sequence [TR8].

**No tradition surveyed names a venous-pressure mechanism.** Ayurveda
localizes vitiated dosha in rectal vessels [TR1]; Unani names pooled
melancholic blood [TR6]; neither maps cleanly onto a hormonal/mechanical
venous-pressure account, and none of the sources opened frame pregnancy
hemorrhoids as distinct from a general aggravation-of-arshas event.

**Iron supplementation: no trace found.** No source opened, across any
tradition, gives iron intake a causal or management role in hemorrhoids.
This is negative space, not an omission from selection.

**Positional offloading: no trace found.** No source opened recommends a
specific body position (side-lying, feet raised) or toilet-sitting duration
as a named hemorrhoid practice; Ayurvedic postpartum regimens address rest
and activity in general terms, not this specific action [TR3].

**Witch hazel's Native attribution is consistently unspecific.** Every
source opened that credits witch hazel's hemorrhoid use to Native American
practice does so without naming a people or a primary record [TR9] — a
recurring vagueness across the secondary literature, not a settled
attribution.

## 5. Translation problems, access failures, and unanswered questions

A modern comprehensive review (Cureus, 2025) lists horse chestnut — a
Balkan-European species — under "Traditional Chinese Medicine" [TR12],
alongside neem and Triphala correctly placed under Ayurveda. No other
source opened gives horse chestnut any Chinese materia medica presence.
This is a documented case of exactly the flattening this map is meant to
avoid: a modern English-language survey merging distinct lineages into one
undifferentiated "traditional herbal remedies" bucket.

Several primary and scholarly documents could not be opened directly:
Avicenna's Canon material rests on secondary review only (TR7,
snippet-only; the Croatian repository copy returned an anti-bot block, and
the Research Gate copy returned 403); a Sushruta Samhita review PDF
(jaims.in) returned corrupted/encoded stream data rather than readable
text; an Ayurvedic pregnancy-constipation source (ijim.co.in) returned an
expired-certificate error. None of the English secondary sources on
Ayurveda give verse-level Sushruta citations — the classical Sanskrit
citation apparatus is consistently absent from secondhand write-ups, itself
a flattening worth naming rather than papering over with an invented
citation.

A preliminary search surfaced a modern Hebrew term, תחתוניות (tachtoniyot),
in discussions of Shabbat medication rules, distinct from the Talmudic
פיקעא (piqa) found directly in Avodah Zarah [TR8]. Whether these are the
same condition under different eras of Hebrew, or different conditions,
could not be resolved from a single retrievable source and is left open.

The oft-repeated claim that Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* describes
*Ruscus aculeatus* could not be corroborated in the pharmacological review
opened [TR10]; it may be true and simply absent from that source, or a
repeated but uncorroborated claim circulating in secondary literature.
