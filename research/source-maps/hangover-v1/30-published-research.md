# Published Research Map — Alcohol Hangover

Commission: what practices might reduce the likelihood or severity of an alcohol
hangover, or make recovery easier, for adults who drink occasionally. See
`00-commission.md` for full boundary.

## 1. Indexes and search routes used

PubMed/MEDLINE, searched both directly (blocked by an interactive cookie wall,
see Section 6) and via the NCBI eutils `efetch` API, which returned usable
abstract text. PubMed Central (PMC) for full-text mirrors of open-access
articles. General web search (not a scholarly index) used to locate candidate
titles, which were then verified against PubMed/PMC/eutils before being
treated as sources. Publisher sites (Wiley Alcoholism: Clinical and
Experimental Research, Springer Psychopharmacology, MDPI Journal of Clinical
Medicine, Journal of Medicinal Food) consulted where linked from PubMed. No
non-English-language index (e.g., CNKI, KISS/RISS, J-STAGE) was searched
directly; any non-Western material found came through English-language
PubMed-indexed papers, not native-language databases — a limitation, not a
finding (Section 6).

## 2. Vocabulary and research terrain

Researchers in this literature use "alcohol hangover" as a defined clinical
term: the cluster of symptoms present the morning after a bout of heavy
drinking, once blood alcohol concentration (BAC) has fallen to zero or near
zero [PR1] [PR2]. Distinct sub-vocabularies recur: "congeners" (non-ethanol,
biologically active compounds produced during fermentation/distillation, e.g.
methanol, higher alcohols, esters) [PR3]; "eBAC" (estimated blood alcohol
concentration, calculated from self-reported consumption rather than measured)
[PR7]; "hangover resistance" or "hangover insensitivity" (reporting no or
minimal symptoms despite heavy intoxication) [PR7]; and instrument names that
function as competing operational definitions of "hangover severity" — the
Acute Hangover Scale (AHS) [PR4], the Alcohol Hangover Severity Scale (AHSS)
[PR5], and (referenced within PR5 and PR7 but not independently retrieved
here) the Hangover Symptom Scale (HSS). "Naturalistic study" is used as a
technical contrast to "randomized controlled trial (RCT)," denoting research
that observes real-world, self-directed drinking rather than a supervised,
dosed drinking session [PR8] [PR9].

## 3. Major research programs or strains of thought

**Mechanism and etiology models.** An older, multifactorial account [PR1]
groups candidate causes into direct alcohol effects (dehydration/diuresis via
antidiuretic hormone suppression, gastrointestinal irritation and delayed
gastric emptying, hypoglycemia, sleep and circadian disruption) and indirect
effects (a mini-withdrawal/rebound hypothesis, acetaldehyde toxicity,
congeners, concurrent drug use, personality traits). A more recent narrative
review [PR2] retains alcohol metabolites and congeners as contributors but
foregrounds neurotransmitter (glutamatergic) changes, neuroinflammation, and
mitochondrial dysfunction as "the most likely factors," while noting that both
human and animal studies in this space are frequently under-controlled.

**Measurement and individual-differences psychometrics.** A cluster of work
treats "how hungover, precisely" as an instrument-design problem rather than a
settled construct. The AHS [PR4] and AHSS [PR5] were built from different
symptom item pools (the AHS from three drinking studies including Swedish
mariners and U.S. students; the AHSS by re-analyzing 47 symptom ratings from
1,410 Dutch students) and report different symptom groupings. A parallel
strain asks who does not get hangovers at all: a literature review of
detoxification-program, survey, and experimental populations estimated that
roughly 23% of people report resisting hangover after intoxication [PR7], and
a separate survey found self-reported immune function was significantly worse
in hangover-susceptible than hangover-resistant drinkers (mean score 10.5 vs
13.1 on an 18-item immune questionnaire, p=0.0001) [PR6].

**Beverage composition (congener) studies.** A crossover RCT dosed healthy
heavy drinkers with bourbon (high-congener) versus vodka (low-congener) versus
placebo, using polysomnography and next-day testing [PR3]. Bourbon produced
worse subjective hangover ratings than vodka, but the two did not differ on
next-day neurocognitive/psychomotor performance, and alcohol's effect on sleep
correlated with hangover severity without statistically mediating the
performance deficits.

**Pharmacological and botanical intervention trials.** A systematic review of
21 randomized placebo-controlled trials (386 participants total) covering
substances including clove extract, red ginseng, Korean pear juice, Hovenia
dulcis, pyritinol, L-cysteine, and tolfenamic acid found that no two trials
tested the same intervention, precluding meta-analysis, and rated all
available evidence "very low quality"; a few substances (clove extract,
tolfenamic acid, pyritinol) were flagged as tolerable enough to warrant
further study [PR12]. This program is large in trial count but fragmented —
each trial is effectively a singleton.

**Food-based and metabolic-support practices.** Two RCTs test food/nutrient
interventions aimed at slowing alcohol absorption or supporting its clearance.
A snack bar formulated with milk protein hydrolysate, almonds, and insoluble
oat fiber reduced peak BAC by roughly 50% relative to fasting in a four-arm
crossover trial (n=21), outperforming an isocaloric savory snack and a larger
mixed meal on a per-calorie basis [PR10]. A separate crossover trial in
ALDH2*1/*1-genotyped Japanese men (n=15 analyzed) found that alanine-fortified
tomato, taken alongside a meal and alcohol, lowered breath alcohol at several
evening timepoints and reduced morning "sedative" symptom scores relative to a
sugar-matched control (5% vs 30% reporting hangover symptoms) [PR11].

**Naturalistic behavioral research on sleep and real-world drinking.** A
wearable-technology study followed 25 social drinkers across an alcohol night
and a control night, finding reduced objective sleep efficiency, lower
self-reported sleep quality, and reduced next-day activity levels after
drinking, and noting that retrospective self-report underestimated actual
consumption [PR9]. A companion methods paper argues explicitly that RCTs and
naturalistic designs answer different questions — RCTs for treatment efficacy
under control, naturalistic designs for real-world quantities, settings, and
functional next-day effects — and that neither substitutes for the other
[PR8].

## 4. Null results, disputes, and neglected questions

- **What counts as "hangover severity" is unresolved.** The AHS [PR4] and
  AHSS [PR5] were built from non-identical item pools and populations, and
  PR5 reports itself as more predictive than comparators without full
  convergence being established here — an open measurement dispute, not a
  settled hierarchy.
- **Hangover-resistance prevalence is not a fixed number.** The ~23%
  resistant figure [PR7] is one estimate; other search results (not
  independently verified as primary studies, so uncited) suggest the figure
  drops sharply at higher confirmed intoxication. The gap tracks how
  "resistance" and "intoxication" get operationalized (self-report vs.
  eBAC), not a factual contradiction.
- **The causal model has shifted without full replacement.** The 1998
  account [PR1] centers dehydration, GI effects, hypoglycemia, and a
  withdrawal-like process; the 2019 account [PR2] centers inflammation and
  mitochondrial dysfunction and explicitly criticizes the human-study base
  as poorly controlled. Both are reviews, not adjudicating trials — no study
  cleanly settles which mechanism dominates.
- **Subjective severity and objective performance dissociate.** In PR3,
  beverage type changed how bad people said they felt without changing how
  well they performed cognitively — a null result against "feeling more
  hungover" tracking "more impaired."
- **Every pharmacological/botanical trial in the largest review is a
  singleton.** PR12 found zero replicated interventions across 21 trials; no
  compelling recommendation is currently supportable from that literature.
- **Naturalistic and controlled evidence do not converge, and the field has
  not resolved which should carry more weight for practice-level questions**
  [PR8] — structural, given this commission's boundary is ordinary
  occasional drinking rather than a lab-dosed session.

## 5. Material, preparation, population, or outcome mismatches

Primary trials in this map skew toward young adult heavy-drinking volunteers
(university students, young professionals, one sample of professional
mariners) dosed to a supervised, often high, BAC in a lab or
clinical-research setting [PR3] [PR4] [PR10] [PR11] — a population and
dosing pattern that only partly overlaps this commission's boundary of
adult *occasional* drinking. Sex representation is uneven across studies
rather than within them: PR6's survey sample was 83% female while congener
and AHS-development trials were predominantly male-recruited; no source
here analyzes sex as a primary variable in severity or response. Genetic
research on ADH1B/ALDH2 is extensive for dependence and cancer risk, and
PR11 selects for the ALDH2*1/*1 genotype specifically, but no dedicated
genome-wide or candidate-gene study of hangover severity as its own
phenotype was located — an absence in this search, not a confirmed gap in
the wider literature. On preparation: trials test isolated extracts,
compounds, or single fortified foods at fixed doses; no trial of a whole
traditional preparation (a multi-ingredient folk soup or formula, as
opposed to an extracted constituent) was located — a search result, not
evidence such trials don't exist elsewhere. On outcome: some sources
measure blood biomarkers (BAC, acetaldehyde, CRP), others subjective
symptom scales, and PR3 shows these need not move together — "what reduces
hangover" depends heavily on which outcome a given study picked.

## 6. Access failures and unanswered questions

Direct WebFetch requests to `pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` article pages returned
an interactive cookie-consent or reCAPTCHA interstitial rather than content;
citations and abstracts were instead retrieved through the NCBI eutils
`efetch` endpoint (plain-text abstract records) and, where available, PMC
full-text mirrors (`pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov`), which render directly. One
publisher link (Springer, PR5) redirected to an institutional-login gateway;
its metadata came via eutils instead. Full text was confirmed for PR1, PR3,
PR6, PR8, PR10, and PR11 (PMC mirrors); PR9 came via search-tool
summarization of the full page rather than a directly rendered mirror
(treated as lower-confidence, not abstract-only). PR2, PR4, PR5, PR7, and
PR12 are marked abstract-only in the index below, retrieved via the eutils
abstract record. A targeted search for a genome-wide association or twin
study isolating hangover severity, as opposed to alcohol consumption or
dependence generally, did not surface a dedicated study; reported as a
result of this session's bounded search, not a confirmed absence in the
wider literature. No non-English-language database (Korean RISS/KISS,
Chinese CNKI, Japanese J-STAGE/CiNii) was searched directly, so research
published only there — plausible given how much Hovenia dulcis, red
ginseng, and kudzu research from Korea is already visible within
English-language PubMed records — is not represented beyond what
English-language indexing surfaces.

## 7. Source index

[PR1] Swift R, Davidson D. "Alcohol hangover: mechanisms and mediators."
*Alcohol Health & Research World*. 1998;22(1):54-60. PMID 15706734.
Accessed 2026-09-02, PMC full-text mirror (PMC6761819). Narrative review.
Studied: synthesizes then-available evidence on candidate mechanisms
(dehydration, GI effects, hypoglycemia, sleep/circadian disruption,
withdrawal-like rebound, acetaldehyde, congeners, personality/drug factors).
Contributes: earliest multifactorial mechanism model in this map; baseline
against which PR2 can be read.

[PR2] Palmer E, Tyacke R, Sastre M, Lingford-Hughes A, Nutt D, Ward RJ.
"Alcohol Hangover: Underlying Biochemical, Inflammatory and Neurochemical
Mechanisms." *Alcohol*. 2019;54(3):196-203. PMID 30916313. Accessed
2026-09-02, eutils abstract record (abstract-only). Narrative review of
animal and human research. Studied: candidate mechanisms — metabolites,
glutamatergic change, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction,
congeners — with explicit critique of the underlying studies' rigor.
Contributes: current mechanism model; names inflammation/mitochondrial
dysfunction as most likely while flagging the evidence base as weak.

[PR3] Rohsenow DJ, Howland J, Arnedt JT, et al. "Intoxication with bourbon
versus vodka: effects on hangover, sleep, and next-day neurocognitive
performance in young adults." *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental
Research*. 2010;34(3):509-518. PMID 20028364. DOI
10.1111/j.1530-0277.2009.01116.x. Accessed 2026-09-02, PMC full-text mirror
(PMC3674844). Randomized crossover trial, n=95 healthy heavy drinkers aged
21-33. Studied: subjective hangover, polysomnography-recorded sleep, and
next-day performance after bourbon, vodka, or placebo. Contributes:
congener content changes reported severity but not measured next-day
performance — a within-study subjective/objective dissociation.

[PR4] Rohsenow DJ, Howland J, Minsky SJ, Greece J, Almeida A, Roehrs TA.
"The Acute Hangover Scale: A new measure of immediate hangover symptoms."
*Addictive Behaviors*. 2007;32(6):1314-1320. PMID 17097819. Accessed
2026-09-02, eutils abstract record (abstract-only). Instrument-development
study pooling three drinking studies (Swedish maritime cadets, n=54; U.S.
students/graduates, n=135). Studied: internal consistency and validity of a
self-report scale administered on waking. Contributes: one of two competing
standardized severity instruments in this map (see PR5).

[PR5] Penning R, McKinney A, Bus LD, Olivier B, Slot K, Verster JC.
"Measurement of alcohol hangover severity: development of the Alcohol
Hangover Severity Scale (AHSS)." *Psychopharmacology*. 2013;225(4):803-810.
PMID 23007602. Accessed 2026-09-02, eutils abstract record (abstract-only;
publisher page redirected to an institutional login gate). Psychometric
study re-analyzing symptom ratings from 1,410 Dutch students plus a
119-participant naturalistic validation sample. Studied: a 12-item severity
scale against existing instruments. Contributes: second, independently
built severity instrument whose item set does not simply replicate PR4 —
the field's unresolved measurement question (Section 4).

[PR6] Van de Loo AJAE, Mackus M, van Schrojenstein Lantman M, et al.
"Susceptibility to Alcohol Hangovers: The Association with Self-Reported
Immune Status." *International Journal of Environmental Research and
Public Health*. 2018;15(6):1286. PMID 29912152. DOI
10.3390/ijerph15061286. Accessed 2026-09-02, PMC full-text mirror.
Cross-sectional online survey, Dutch students aged 18-30, n=481 analyzed
of 2,295 respondents. Studied: self-reported immune function compared
between hangover-sensitive (n=400) and -resistant (n=81) drinkers.
Contributes: individual-differences strand — lower self-rated immune
function in sensitive drinkers as a candidate explanation.

[PR7] Howland J, Rohsenow DJ, Edwards EM. "Are some drinkers resistant to
hangover? A literature review." *Current Drug Abuse Reviews*.
2008;1(1):42-46. PMID 19630704. Accessed 2026-09-02, eutils abstract
record (abstract-only). Literature review across detox-program, community,
student, and heavy-drinker populations. Studied: reported prevalence and
predictors of hangover resistance. Contributes: source of the widely cited
~23% resistance estimate; frames resistance as understudied and possibly
behaviorally consequential.

[PR8] Verster JC, et al. "Advantages and Limitations of Naturalistic Study
Designs and Their Implementation in Alcohol Hangover Research." *Journal
of Clinical Medicine*. 2019;8(12):2160. PMID 31817752. DOI
10.3390/jcm8122160. Accessed 2026-09-02, PMC full-text mirror (PMC6947227).
Methods/argument paper, not a primary study. Studied: comparative strengths
and weaknesses of naturalistic vs. RCT designs (recall bias, dropout,
confounding vs. poor ecological validity). Contributes: names the field's
core methodological dispute (Section 4) directly.

[PR9] Devenney LE, Coyle KB, Roth T, Verster JC. "Sleep after Heavy Alcohol
Consumption and Physical Activity Levels during Alcohol Hangover." *Journal
of Clinical Medicine*. 2019;8(5):752. PMID 31137775. Accessed 2026-09-02
via search-tool summarization rather than a directly rendered page (lower
confidence than a directly rendered PMC page; not marked abstract-only).
Naturalistic wearable-technology study, n=25 social drinkers, alcohol vs.
control night. Studied: objective sleep efficiency, subjective sleep
quality, next-day activity via wearables plus self-report. Contributes:
evidence that drinking reduces sleep quality and next-day activity, and
that retrospective self-report underestimates consumption.

[PR10] Fisher JM, Wolever TMS, Campbell JE, et al. "Effect of a Snack Bar
Optimized to Reduce Alcohol Bioavailability: A Randomized Controlled
Clinical Trial in Healthy Individuals." *Journal of Medicinal Food*.
2020;23(4):432-439. PMID 31755823. DOI 10.1089/jmf.2019.0228. Accessed
2026-09-02, PMC full-text mirror (PMC7185312). Randomized four-way
crossover trial, n=21 healthy adults. Studied: peak BAC and bioavailability
after a fiber/protein snack bar vs. a savory snack, a larger meal, or
fasting, before a standardized dose. Contributes: quantified evidence for a
food-timing/composition practice, more directly practice-relevant than
older general food-and-BAC pharmacokinetic work.

[PR11] Oshima S, Shiiya S, Tokumaru Y, Kanda T. "Alanine-fortified tomatoes
relieve the acute alcohol-induced adverse effects in healthy men: a
randomized cross-over study." *Nutrition & Metabolism*. 2016;13:25. PMID
27051456. DOI 10.1186/s12986-016-0087-9. Accessed 2026-09-02, PMC
full-text mirror (PMC4820955). Randomized crossover trial, Japanese men
genotyped ALDH2*1/*1, n=15 analyzed of 20 enrolled. Studied: breath alcohol
and next-morning "sedative" symptom scores after alanine-fortified tomato
vs. sugar-matched control, with a standardized dose and meal. Contributes:
non-Western, genotype-selected population; primary evidence for a specific
food-based practice (5% vs 30% reporting hangover symptoms).

[PR12] Roberts E, Smith R, Hotopf M, Drummond C (author list per the
retrieval source used; a separate bibliography lists additional
