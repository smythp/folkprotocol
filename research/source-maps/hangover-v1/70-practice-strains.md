# Practice-Strain Field — Hangover Prevention and Recovery

## How this field was reconstructed

This field draws only on the seven completed inputs in this directory: the commission and the six source maps (reddit/forums, traditional, published research, commercial web, experimental communities, practitioner/folk web). No raw source, web search, or outside memory was consulted for this pass. An internal inventory of every concrete action described or implied across the six maps was built first, including actions buried inside source summaries or offered with an implausible folk explanation, then grouped by shared operative move or native logic rather than by source domain, ingredient, or map of origin. Named traditions, incompatible rationales, and internal disputes are nested within each strain rather than flattened or promoted to separate entries. Citations use the six maps' own local IDs; nothing here is cited that is absent from those maps.

## Broad practice strains

### 1. Consumption-phase pacing and absorption-shaping

Intervene in what, how fast, and alongside what during the drinking episode, rather than treating symptoms afterward. Eating a substantial meal, hydrating alongside alcohol, and pacing drinks recur from the oldest thread found (2004) through the newest [10:RF1][10:RF2][10:RF3]. Drink-type selection recurs twice: an RCT found high-congener bourbon produced worse subjective hangover than low-congener vodka though the two did not differ on next-day objective performance [30:PR3]; a biohacker blog ranks alcohol types by presumed toxin load and recommends avoiding the "worst" categories [50:EX6]. Two RCTs engineer absorption directly: a snack bar (milk protein hydrolysate, almonds, oat fiber) cut peak BAC roughly 50% versus fasting [30:PR10], and alanine-fortified tomato taken with a meal and alcohol lowered breath alcohol and morning symptom scores in ALDH2*1/*1-genotyped Japanese men [30:PR11]. Harm-reduction wikis port dosing apparatus onto alcohol: TripSit's unit-conversion formula and PsychonautWiki's dosing tiers and BAC thresholds [50:EX2][50:EX3]. A German blog treats reduced overall amount as a value, without a specific technique attached, inside a tradition that also doubts its own signature remedy [60:PF6].

Footprint: cross-map (10, 30, 50, 60) — forum tips, RCT evidence, biohacker advice, harm-reduction wiki apparatus. Broad but uneven: forum and wiki material states pacing as folk-certain, while the RCT evidence covers specific compounds and populations and does not validate that general framing.

Dispute: PR3's dissociation — congener choice changed how bad people said they felt but not how well they performed — warns that feeling better and testing better are not the same outcome. Separately, the experimental-communities map's own synthesis notes that a toxin-tier drink-ranking model and a pure-dose/combination model are two different causal stories about what a hangover is, not two versions of one theory (50-experimental-communities §4, point 3).

### 2. Oral rehydration and electrolyte repletion

Replace fluid and minerals alcohol is believed to deplete, taken as both a preventive and a morning-after fix. Plain water or a branded electrolyte drink (Gatorade, Dioralyte, Berocca) is offered across every forum read directly, often as settled fact [10:RF1][10:RF2][10:RF4]. The same baseline recurs across otherwise-disagreeing experimental communities: water-per-drink on Bluelight, alternating water with alcohol on TripSit, PsychonautWiki's "rehydration therapy," and Shroomery skeptics falling back on hydration as the one intervention they trust [50:EX1][50:EX2][50:EX3][50:EX4]. Folk mineral-replenishment framing appears inside specific drinks: the Bloody Mary's salt/fructose/potassium/vitamin-C rationale, argued to have "a medical basis" [60:PF9]; the Mexican michelada's Clamato-and-lime "electrolytes" claim, flagged by its own writer as unproven [60:PF4]; a German blog's endorsement of sparkling water [60:PF6]. A sea-moss vendor markets sea moss for hangover minerals, candidly framed as "the growing buzz" rather than inherited tradition, against a household Trinidadian source describing the same drink without ever mentioning alcohol or hangover [60:PF11][60:PF12].

Footprint: cross-map (10, 50, 60) — forum consensus, harm-reduction doctrine, food-culture journalism, one commercial blog. Broad in repetition, narrow in verification: no source here measures whether rehydration changes outcomes, and one Bluelight reply explicitly challenges dehydration as "one small aspect" rather than the master explanation, echoed by an unresolved internal dispute on the same point in the experimental-communities map [10:RF2][50:EX1].

Dispute: whether dehydration is primary or partial is contested inside single threads on two maps, not resolved by either. The sea-moss pairing is this field's clearest instance of a hangover claim living only in a commercial rendering of an otherwise-real household practice [60:PF11][60:PF12].

### 3. Bypass-digestion delivery: IV infusion and transdermal patch

Deliver fluids, vitamins, or medication by a route other than swallowing, on the theory that alcohol's gastrointestinal effects make oral products unreliable. IV clinics sell a "Myers' Cocktail" (B12, B-complex, magnesium, glutathione, vitamin C in saline) — a genericized 1954 formula offered independently by at least two unaffiliated clinics [40:CW2][40:CW8]. A transdermal patch's founding narrative argues oral pills are "ineffective due to alcohol['s] interference with digestive absorption" [40:CW7]. Forum discussion treats IV drips as a newer, paid-for practice distinct from home remedies [10:RF9][10:RF10].

Footprint: the commercial-web map carries direct vendor material (two clinics, one patch); the reddit-forums map carries only outside commentary and one outcome report. No independent pharmacokinetic or efficacy data for either format was located — the absorption-bypass claim is asserted by sellers, not evidenced by a third party (40-commercial-web §4).

Dispute: one community calls outside-clinical IV hydration a "scam" in the abstract [10:RF9]; a separate poster reports paying $220 and would not repeat it [10:RF10]. Regulatory dispute: the FDA sent warning letters in 2020 to IV-clinic and supplement sellers, including one clinic mapped here, over cure/prevention language; that clinic's site still read "proven to treat and cure hangovers fast" six years later [40:CW1][40:CW2].

### 4. Hair of the dog / same-substance re-dosing

Treat the hangover by consuming more alcohol, or a ritually alcohol-adjacent preparation, on a like-cures-like logic. The oldest attestation is Bronze Age: a Ugaritic text has the god ʾIlu treat his own hangover with a salve of dog hair and a plant in olive oil, read against Pliny's later "like cures like" framing; the English phrase is attested from 1546 and reached its modern drinking sense by the 19th century [20:TR7]. Ayurveda holds an independent textual cousin: madātyaya from improper drinking "pacified by consumption of the same [liquor] in appropriate quantity" [20:TR3]. The American prairie oyster (raw egg, vinegar, Worcestershire, hot sauce) is grouped under this same lineage heading even though the recipe contains no alcohol — an inconsistency in how the tradition is bounded, left unresolved here [20:TR7][20:TR8]. The Bloody Mary functions as an institutionalized version, argued by a drinks-history newsletter to have "a medical basis" [60:PF9]; a German term, Konterbier ("counter-beer"), names drinking again the next day, surfaced by a food-science podcast that frames the category as crowdsourced listener wisdom rather than one authoritative practice [60:PF10, snippet-only]. Forum posters raise the practice unprompted and dispute whether it relieves symptoms or only delays them, using withdrawal-style language for what a hangover is [10:RF4][10:RF6].

Footprint: cross-map and long-lineage (10, 20, 60) — a Bronze Age text, a classical Sanskrit verse, an American folk recipe, forum threads, and drinks journalism. Broad across unconnected traditions, but never tested in this pass by any source outside folk or journalistic register.

Dispute: one Student Room poster claims a paper found it "does actually cure" a hangover; another counters it only postpones withdrawal-type symptoms; a third grants relief while flagging liver harm [10:RF6]. A home-bartending blog calls the prairie oyster "the most awful drink I've ever made" and doubts its own recipe, directly opposed to the drinks-history newsletter's defense of the category [60:PF2][60:PF9].

### 5. Restorative next-day dish and drink traditions

Eat or drink a specific, often salty, sour, spicy, or fatty preparation the morning after, each explained through its own folk-physiological reasoning. Korean haejang-guk ("release" + "viscera") — hot soup of meat, noodles, scallions, historically Sichuan-pepper — is documented through a language-teaching text and later poetry and genre painting rather than the formal medical canon [20:TR2], and independently confirmed as literally "hangover soup" by a Korean-American essay that widens the comparison to Polish horseradish soup, Chinese congee, and Czech garlic soup under a shared capsaicin-sweating theory ("release the nasty hangover toxins") [60:PF7]. Japanese miso soup with shijimi clams restores liver function, and umeboshi (salted, soured plum) eases nausea and "systemic toxicity," per folk belief [20:TR6]. A 1635 Russian travel account records cold lamb, pepper, and cucumber in vinegar and pickle brine; rassol (pickle brine) is drunk directly or as rassolnik soup [20:TR5]. Jewish-American Purim breakfasts (shakshuka, chopped liver, challah French toast) are reasoned as "eggs = protein = recovery" [60:PF3]. German Katerfrühstück centers pickled herring (rollmops) and gherkins for sodium/potassium, though the source calls the herring cure "one of those comedy suggestions people give to the hungover" even while endorsing it [60:PF6]. Mexican menudo, pozole, and michelada carry a saying that fancier drinks mean less hangover [60:PF4]. Reddit staples include greasy fry-ups, eggs, fast food, carb-heavy meals, and one poster's pick of spicy curry to "soak up" the night [10:RF5][10:RF6].

Footprint: cross-map and cross-cultural (10, 20, 60) — encyclopedic and classical sources, independent food-culture journalism, and forum posts. Broad by repetition of shape (a culturally load-bearing dish) across unconnected traditions, though each gives its own mechanism and none is tested against another; the traditional map separately names an acid/salt-correction thread (rassol, umeboshi, prairie-oyster vinegar) that several of these dishes also belong to (20-traditional §3).

Dispute/variant: the German source is the only instance here of a writer immersed in a tradition openly doubting one of its own remedies while still endorsing simpler adjacent ones [60:PF6].

### 6. Differentiated humoral/constitutional medicine

Diagnose the drinker's constitution, or the wine's own temperament, before applying a specific — sometimes opposite — remedy, refusing one universal treatment. Caraka Samhita differentiates Vata-, Pitta-, and Kapha-dominant intoxication by cause and symptom: warming oils and meat broth for Vata, cooling grape juice and sandal paste for Pitta, emesis and heating spices for Kapha [20:TR3]. Perso-Islamic/Galenic medicine as practiced in Iran varies wine's effect and remedy by temperament and the wine's own qualities, with named physicians (Aghili Khorasani, Arzani, Jorjani, Avicenna) recommending rosewater syrup, pomegranate-pear juice, wormwood syrup, or vinegar-water, and showing "considerable disagreement" on safe quantities [20:TR4].

Footprint: narrow to the traditional map alone (20), but internally rich — a primary-text translation and a secondary historical review — and distinct from every other strain here: no other map differentiates practice by individual diagnosis before treatment.

Dispute: internal, not cross-source. Any modern gloss reducing madātyaya to one practice erases a real opposition (warming versus cooling by cause); the Persian material shows physicians disagreeing rather than converging on one protocol [20:TR3][20:TR4].

### 7. Multi-ingredient compounded formulas

Combine many mechanistically distinct ingredients into one preparation or protocol, addressing several presumed causes at once. The Chinese formula Ge Hua Jie Cheng San (thirteen herbs: kudzu flower as chief, plus cardamom, aucklandia, citrus peels, poria, atractylodes, ginseng, and others) targets a named pattern, Spleen-Stomach damp-heat, not "hangover" generically; secondary literature is flagged as extracting kudzu flower or Hovenia dulcis from this formula as if either were a standalone active ingredient [20:TR1]. A Scottish herbalist's first-person Recovery Tea (wood betony, meadowsweet, mint, chamomile, poppy, rosemary, honey, lemon) substitutes meadowsweet for willow bark on practical grounds, alongside folk-magic lore from the same tradition [60:PF1]. A "certified herbalist" blog lists ten hangover herbs in clinical-sounding language inside a page monetized by affiliate links and a promoted capsule product — the clearest instance here of credential framing combined with commercial entanglement [60:PF8]. Commercial kitchen-sink capsules stack DHM, milk thistle, activated charcoal, apple cider vinegar, vitamin C, B vitamins, ginseng, and prickly pear in one SKU [40:CW12]; a patented product pairs DHM with NAC at a set minimum ratio, with prickly pear among optional additions [40:CW5][40:CW6]. Biohacker stacks include a toxin-tier drink ranking paired with charcoal, glutathione, B vitamins, alpha lipoic acid, and NAC [50:EX6], and a four-pillar stack (liver support, toxin binders, mitochondrial support, electrolyte drops) timed before drinking, before bed, and the morning after [50:EX7]; nootropics-community "stacking" culture supplies the same combinatorial logic plus an internal critique acknowledging pseudonymous single-subject testing cannot be a real blind trial [50:EX5].

Footprint: cross-map (20, 40, 50, 60). Broad in repetition of the compounding logic; ingredient sets recur across independently branded products in a way the commercial map cannot distinguish shared ancestry from copying (40-commercial-web §5).

Dispute: prickly pear recurs as a commercial ingredient here [40:CW6][40:CW12] alongside a separate finding that its popular "Aztec hangover cure" framing is unsupported by primary sources — documented Aztec material concerns drinking-limit law, not a cactus remedy [20:TR9][20:TR11]. A ranking site's own dosing rubric implicitly indicts most of this strain's commercial branch for underdosing ingredients other than its preferred compound [40:CW9], connecting directly to strain 8.

### 8. Single targeted compound or metabolic-pathway intervention

Isolate one compound or organism and target one specific biochemical pathway, instead of compounding many ingredients together. DHM is the flagship, sold in capsules and drinks and framed as speeding acetaldehyde clearance via liver enzymes [40:CW9][40:CW11]; one forum register calls it "clinically proven," while another cites a peer-reviewed analysis of 82 hangover products finding no human safety or efficacy data for it despite that reputation [10:RF7][10:RF8]; a ranking site sets an independent dosing bar (300–600 mg, "1000mg ideal") and flags undisclosed "proprietary blends" and sub-300mg doses as red flags [40:CW9]. A patented DHM-plus-NAC pairing claims cysteine binds acetaldehyde into "a non-toxic compound called MTCA," distinct from DHM's own mechanism [40:CW5][40:CW6]. ZBiotics, an engineered *Bacillus subtilis* strain, is marketed to degrade acetaldehyde in the gut rather than the liver, with an explicit disclaimer that it does not affect blood alcohol or intoxication [40:CW3]. Blowfish, an FDA drug-facts-labeled aspirin (500 mg) plus caffeine (60 mg) tablet, makes no acetaldehyde or prevention claim, only "temporary relief" after the fact [40:CW4]. A systematic review of 21 placebo-controlled RCTs (386 participants: clove extract, red ginseng, Korean pear juice, Hovenia dulcis, pyritinol, L-cysteine, tolfenamic acid) found no two trials tested the same intervention and rated all evidence "very low quality," though three were flagged as tolerable enough to warrant further study [30:PR12]. Hacker News commentary converges on DHM and ZBiotics as the two products commenters reported actually noticing an effect from [50:EX12].

Footprint: cross-map (10, 30, 40, 50) — forum claims, a systematic review of trials, vendor pages, one federal drug label, tech-forum commentary. The published-research and commercial-web footprints disagree in kind: the trial literature treats every compound as an unreplicated singleton; the commercial layer treats DHM as an established, dosable ingredient.

Dispute: DHM's evidence status is directly contested within the reddit-forums map itself [10:RF7][10:RF8], and three incompatible mechanism stories — liver-enzyme (DHM), direct acetaldehyde-binding (NAC), gut-bacterial (ZBiotics) — are sold side by side with no source comparing them head-to-head (40-commercial-web §4).

### 9. Physiological activation and passive recovery

Force or allow a change in circulation, temperature, exertion, breathing, or arousal state, rather than administering a substance. A single full-thread capture repeatedly recommends running, hard cardio, and stair-stepper work, or simply "keep moving" despite an unpleasant start; several replies pair this with sauna or infrared sauna and hot or cold showering; sleep is separately listed among recovery steps in the same thread [10:RF12]. Posters commonly explain the effect as "sweating out toxins," but the map's own analysis treats the recurrence of the practice as belonging to it even where that explanation does not (10-reddit-forums §3, S7).

Footprint: narrow — this strain rests on one full-thread source in one map (10), a 353-comment capture where the practice recurs across many replies. No other map documents exercise, heat, or cold exposure as a hangover practice.

Dispute: none recorded. The source's own framing separates this from passive hydration and restorative food as a distinct operative move, the basis for treating it as its own strain rather than folding it into either.

### 10. Symptom-targeted substance relief, licit and illicit register

Take a substance chosen to counter a specific felt symptom directly, rather than address a presumed underlying cause. Generic OTC painkiller advice appears alongside a caution, on the same page, against combining certain painkillers with alcohol due to liver risk [10:RF1, D2]. Blowfish sells an aspirin-plus-caffeine effervescent for post-hoc headache and fatigue relief, with no prevention claim [40:CW4]. An older harm-reduction register names prescription and recreational drugs — benzodiazepines, gabapentin, cannabis — alongside food and water, ranked by the source itself: food and water first, then weed, "lastly benzos... too many negative side effects" [10:RF3]; a separate capture repeats cannabis and stimulants among its recovery suggestions [10:RF12].

Footprint: dual-map (10, 40) — forum advice spanning two registers plus one FDA-labeled product. No source here claims this approach addresses alcohol's own metabolism; each frames it as symptom management only.

Dispute: none direct, but RF3's own ranking of its named substances by acceptability is stated by the source, not inferred here.

## Unclustered directions

1. **Self-quantification and personal-threshold tracking.** Two Quantified Self forum threads treat hangover-adjacent drinking as an instrumentation problem: one wants timestamped drink-logging correlated with sleep and health-app data to find what is driving insomnia [50:EX8]; an earlier thread discusses a wearable blood-alcohol sensor framed around finding a "minimum effective dose" without the hangover downside [50:EX9] — that device's later crowdfunded consumer version was marked refunded, with no explanation retrievable [50:EX10]. Gwern's year-long self-experiment sits in the same lineage, though it targeted sleep quality rather than hangover severity, and returned no measurable correlation at the author's own consumption levels [50:EX11]. Confined to a single map, this is a different kind of thing than every strain above — a method for learning a personal pattern, not a remedy applied on the assumption that it works.

2. **Drinking-limit regulation through law and ritual.** The only Aztec material located concerns pulque hedged with a "fifth cup" threshold said to guarantee bad behavior, restricted mostly to nobles, priests, and the elderly, with penalties documented in the Florentine Codex, Codex Mendoza, and the chronicles of Sahagún and Durán [20:TR9]. The traditional map's own analysis states this is social and legal regulation of drinking itself, not a recovery practice, and does not map onto a next-day intervention — preserved here rather than folded into a remedy-shaped strain.

3. **Folk-magic transference ritual.** A Scottish folk-herbalism blog records practices of transferring the affliction rather than treating the body — rubbing a hangover onto a slug, or cursing a bottle's cork with thirteen pins — alongside willow bark and bitter-vetch [60:PF1]. This single-source mention's operative logic, sympathetic transference to another object or being, is categorically different from every ingestion- or exertion-based strain here.

4. **Psychedelic microdosing as post-drinking symptom mitigation.** One Shroomery thread describes using small doses of psilocybin (roughly 0.1–0.25g) or LSD after drinking to blunt hangover symptoms, with one reply proposing a further combination (LSD plus ibuprofen) and a skeptic in the same thread stating flatly that "a true hangover cure will make its discoverer a very rich man" [50:EX4]. This rests on one thread with roughly two affirmative accounts against one explicit rebuttal; its substance class and intent do not fit strain 10's symptom-targeting or strains 7–8's toxin-clearance logic closely enough to fold in.

## Coverage and boundary note

Several parts of the mapped territory contain no practice and are recorded here as negative space rather than converted into one. Reddit was categorically unreachable beyond search snippets and one supplied capture, across two maps (10-reddit-forums §5; 50-experimental-communities §5), limiting confidence in how broad several forum-level claims really are. Erowid's hangover page and an Appalachian folk-remedy blog were both blocked outright (403) and are not represented (50-experimental-communities §5; 60-practitioner-folk-web §6, [60:PF-AF1]). Donguibogam was searched specifically for a hangover passage and none was found [20:TR10]; the Ozark folklore archive was reread to confirm it holds no hangover entry among dozens of other remedies — a genuine absence, not a search-depth failure [60:PF5]. A Nigerian agbo blog (expired certificate), three herbal-education organizations with no hangover-specific content, and a podcast transcript available only as a snippet [60:PF10] may hold further practices never seen. forum.bodybuilding.com and Longecity were both blocked in two maps (10-reddit-forums §5; 50-experimental-communities §5). Sub-Saharan African, Southeast Asian, Caribbean, and Talmudic material was largely unsearched rather than searched-and-absent (20-traditional §1, §5), and no non-English research database was searched despite visible upstream influence on English-indexed trial ingredients (30-published-research §6). Mechanism and measurement research — etiology reviews, severity-scale instruments, resistance-prevalence estimates, and immune-status or sleep-tracking studies [30:PR1][30:PR2][30:PR4][30:PR5][30:PR6][30:PR7][30:PR9] — measures hangover without constituting a practice, and is not represented as one here. "Hangxiety" [10:RF11] is a named consequence and a reason some posters report drinking less overall, not a technique applied before, during, or after drinking, and sits outside this field's boundary on the reddit-forums map's own terms (10-reddit-forums §4, D6).

This pass did not rank, score, or evaluate any strain, manifestation, or unclustered direction for safety, efficacy, or suitability, and did not design any dose, schedule, test, or protocol; grouping reflects shared operative logic only, drawn from what the six maps and the commission document as done or claimed. Disputes recorded within a strain — hair-of-the-dog's cure-versus-delay disagreement, DHM's clinically-proven-versus-no-human-data rebuttal, dehydration-as-primary-versus-partial-cause, and the incompatible mechanism stories sold side by side in the supplement market — are left exactly as open as the source maps left them.
