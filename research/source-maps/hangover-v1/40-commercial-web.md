# Commercial Web Map — Hangover Prevention/Recovery

## 1. Search routes and market segments visited

Routes: direct product searches ("best hangover pills 2026", "DHM supplement"), brand-name searches (ZBiotics, Flyby, Morning Recovery/More Labs, Cheers Health, Bytox, Blowfish), delivery-format searches ("hangover patch", "hangover IV therapy"), regulatory searches (FDA/FTC warning letters, industry self-regulation challenges), patent searches (USPTO/Google Patents for hangover compositions), and review/complaint searches (Reddit, Trustpilot, retailer reviews).

Segments covered: oral capsule/tablet supplements (Flyby, Cheers Restore, DHM Guide-ranked brands), ready-to-drink liquid supplements (Morning Recovery), an engineered probiotic drink (ZBiotics), an FDA-monograph OTC analgesic tablet (Blowfish), transdermal patches (Bytox), in-person and mobile IV infusion clinics (Hangover Heaven, Fluid Revival), an affiliate review/ranking site (DHM Guide), a trade-press account of industry self-regulation (Nutrition Insight/CRN), and one federal regulatory action (FDA warning letters). Not reached in this pass: prescription-adjacent "hangover shot" injectables, activated-charcoal-only single-ingredient sellers, and non-US markets (Korean/Japanese OTC hangover product pages were referenced only secondhand via US retailers).

## 2. Product and claim vocabulary

Recurring nouns: "recovery," "restore," "prevention," "detox," "bounce back," "wake up like you drank half." Mechanism vocabulary clusters into two families — metabolic ("acetaldehyde," "ALDH/ADH enzymes," "liver support," "alcohol clearance") and inflammatory/GABA ("GABA rebound," "inflammation," "oxidative stress"). Formulation vocabulary: "clinically studied," "patented," "no proprietary blends," "physician formulated," "FDA-registered facility" (a manufacturing-compliance claim, distinct from FDA approval of the product itself). Guarantee vocabulary: "money-back guarantee," "satisfaction guaranteed." Delivery-format vocabulary: "bypasses digestion" (patches, IV), "engineered probiotic" (ZBiotics), "fast-acting" (nearly universal). Dosing-window vocabulary splits sharply into "before/during" and "morning after" framing, mirrored from the metabolic-timing logic also present in Folkprotocol's own prior hangover page.

## 3. Recurring formulation and narrative families

**DHM (dihydromyricetin, from Hovenia dulcis) as flagship ingredient.** Present across capsule brands (Flyby [CW12], DHM Guide-ranked products [CW9]) and drink brands (Morning Recovery [CW11]). Narrative: DHM "speeds up" alcohol/acetaldehyde clearance via liver enzymes. DHM Guide sets a vendor-independent dosing bar of 300–600 mg, "1000mg ideal" [CW9].

**DHM + NAC/cysteine dual-mechanism, IP-protected.** Cheers Health's Restore product and its own US patent both describe a "patented dual action formula": DHM said to reduce "GABA rebound," cysteine said to bind acetaldehyde into a "non-toxic compound called MTCA" [CW5]. The patent claims a specific DHM:NAC weight ratio of at least 3:1 with named optional additions (prickly pear, milk thistle, B vitamins) [CW6].

**Kitchen-sink multi-ingredient capsules.** Flyby's formula stacks DHM, milk thistle, activated charcoal, apple cider vinegar, vitamin C, B vitamins, ginseng, and prickly pear in one capsule, marketed as "Take Before and After a Night Out" [CW12]. This pattern — many mechanistically distinct ingredients in one SKU — recurs across most capsule brands surfaced by DHM Guide's roundup.

**Gut-mechanism engineered probiotic.** ZBiotics markets a categorically different story: a genetically engineered *Bacillus subtilis* strain that degrades acetaldehyde in the gut rather than the liver, with an explicit disclaimer that it "will not affect your level of intoxication" or blood alcohol level [CW3]. This is the only source in this map describing a live-organism, patented-strain mechanism rather than a plant extract or vitamin blend.

**OTC analgesic/stimulant, drug-monograph route.** Blowfish (aspirin 500 mg + caffeine 60 mg) is registered as an FDA drug-facts-labeled product via DailyMed, not marketed as a dietary supplement, and makes no acetaldehyde or prevention claims — only "temporary relief" of headache and fatigue symptoms after the fact [CW4]. This is a structurally different regulatory posture from every supplement brand in this map.

**Bypass-digestion delivery formats: patches and IV.** Bytox's founding narrative explicitly frames its transdermal patch as a response to the belief that oral pills are "ineffective due to alcohol's interference with digestive absorption" [CW7]. IV clinics make the same bypass-digestion argument for infused vitamins/fluids: Fluid Revival's Myers' Cocktail (B12, B-complex, magnesium, glutathione, vitamin C in 1L saline, $279) [CW8] and Hangover Heaven's three tiered IV packages plus vitamin shots [CW2] both sell direct-delivery as inherently superior to oral routes.

## 4. Disagreements, customer-reported failures, and negative space

**FDA vs. the supplement category on cure/prevention language.** In 2020 the FDA sent warning letters to at least seven hangover-supplement sellers, including Hangover Heaven, for claims like "Hangover Cure through Prevention" and "much less of a hangover the next morning" — the agency's position is that any hangover-prevention/treatment claim makes the product an unapproved drug, and separately flagged NAC as an ingredient with a pre-existing drug approval that bars its supplement use [CW1].

**That warning appears not to have changed marketing durably.** Hangover Heaven's own site, checked six years after its 2020 letter, still reads "proven to treat and cure hangovers fast" [CW2] — a claim in the same register the FDA objected to. This map cannot confirm the current regulatory status of that specific claim; it is presented as an observed discrepancy, not a finding about enforcement outcomes.

**Industry self-regulation disagreement, resolved by claim-narrowing.** The Council for Responsible Nutrition (a trade body, not a regulator) challenged More Labs' claim that Morning Recovery was "scientifically formulated to help you bounce back after drinking," on the grounds that only 4 of 6 measured symptoms hit the ~80% improvement figure being implied broadly. More Labs revised the claim to name the specific symptoms (headache, heart-pounding, concentration, clumsiness) rather than imply blanket effectiveness [CW10]. More Labs' current product page still leads with the "up to 80% better" figure [CW11], now scoped to "specific symptoms."

**A review site's own ranking implicitly indicts most of the category on dosing.** DHM Guide names "proprietary blends" (undisclosed individual doses) and doses below 300 mg as red flags, and states DHM is "the only ingredient with multiple peer-reviewed human and animal studies" among those it surveyed [CW9] — an implicit claim that supporting ingredients (milk thistle, ginger, vitamins) common across the category lack comparable evidence, even though those same ingredients appear in the site's own top-ranked products.

**Two incompatible mechanism stories are sold side by side without cross-reference.** ZBiotics is explicit that its product does not touch blood alcohol or liver metabolism [CW3], while DHM- and NAC-based products claim to speed alcohol/acetaldehyde clearance through liver-linked pathways [CW5][CW6][CW9][CW11]. Nothing found in this pass compares the two mechanism families head-to-head or flags the disagreement to a shopper choosing between them.

**Absorption claims for bypass-digestion formats are asserted, not evidenced, in the sources reached.** Bytox's origin story argues patches beat pills because "alcohol['s] interference with digestive absorption" makes oral products "ineffective" [CW7], but the source (a Wikipedia article, not a company or clinical page) presents this as company rationale without engaging pharmacokinetic evidence for or against transdermal B-vitamin absorption. No independent efficacy or absorption data for the patch format was located in this pass.

## 5. Copying, ownership, affiliate, and incentive notes

Cheers Health owns both the Restore product's marketing narrative and US Patent 11717507B2, filed November 2022 and granted August 2023, which claims the specific DHM:NAC formulation and ratio underlying the product's "dual action" pitch [CW5][CW6]. The marketing and the legal claim are the same company's IP, not independent validation of each other.

DHM Guide functions as a review/ranking hub (it states it "tested 12 of the most popular hangover prevention supplements") that links out to branded purchase pages and ranks products against its own dosage rubric [CW9]. No affiliate-disclosure language was visible on the specific page fetched; the business model (product rankings plus purchase links) is typical of affiliate content, but this map cannot confirm a financial relationship from the page content alone.

Ingredient vocabulary converges tightly across independently-branded capsule and drink products — DHM, milk thistle, prickly pear, B vitamins, electrolytes, ginseng recur across Flyby [CW12], Morning Recovery [CW11], Cheers [CW5], and the products DHM Guide ranks [CW9] — consistent with either a shared ancestor formula (the Ge Hua Jie Cheng San tradition referenced in Folkprotocol's own prior page) or straightforward mutual copying within a crowded DTC category; this pass cannot distinguish the two.

"Myers' Cocktail" itself is a genericized term (originating with Dr. John Myers, 1954) that multiple unaffiliated IV clinics — Hangover Heaven [CW2] and Fluid Revival [CW8] — use for near-identical B-vitamin/magnesium/vitamin-C infusion products, so convergence here reflects a shared public-domain formula name rather than copying between these two specific businesses.

## 6. Access failures and unanswered questions

Flyby's own brand domain returned only page-navigation markup on fetch, not product content; ingredient and dosing detail for Flyby in this map comes from a partial direct read of its Amazon listing [CW12] plus corroborating detail visible in search-engine result summaries that were not independently re-verified on a single fetched page — treat the specific milligram figures (300 mg DHM, 200 mg milk thistle) as secondhand relative to this map's direct-access standard.

Walmart's and Sam's Club's product/review pages returned bot-verification screens to WebFetch; no customer review text could be captured directly, so this map has no first-person customer-complaint source, only the trade-press and reviewer-site material in section 4.

A Medium article reporting an AI-driven sentiment analysis of hangover-pill reviews (majority positive, "inefficacy" as the leading complaint category) returned an HTTP 403 on fetch and could not be verified against source text; it is omitted from the source index rather than cited without a checkable excerpt.

The scanned PDF of Cheers Health's own patent filing (uspto.gov image server) could not be parsed as text; a Google Patents HTML mirror was used instead for CW6.

Unanswered: whether any bypass-digestion delivery format (patch or IV) has publicly available pharmacokinetic data specific to hangover-relevant compounds; how the FDA's 2020 warning letters affected the letter recipients' marketing durably (Hangover Heaven's current claims suggest not, for at least one recipient); and how many of the ingredient-convergent capsule brands trace to the same contract manufacturer versus independent formulation.
