# Traditional Map — Hangover Prevention and Recovery

## 1. Traditions and search routes visited

Eight named lineages were searched directly rather than as one "traditional" bucket: Chinese classical formula medicine (Jin-Yuan dynasty formula literature) [TR1]; Korean food-and-medicine tradition (haejang-guk lineage and the Donguibogam medical canon) [TR2][TR10]; Ayurveda (Caraka Samhita's madatyaya doctrine) [TR3]; Perso-Islamic/Galenic medicine as practiced in Iran (Avicenna's Canon and later physicians) [TR4]; Russian/Slavic tavern-and-household folk practice [TR5]; Japanese household folk remedy [TR6]; the Anglo-European-American "hair of the dog" / prairie oyster lineage, traced from Bronze Age Ugarit through early modern Europe to the American frontier [TR7][TR8]; and Mesoamerican/Aztec legal-ritual regulation of drunkenness [TR9][TR11]. The existing Folkprotocol hangover page and its bibliography were read as prior material per the commission, not as a source of authority; where they overlap with a tradition below, that is noted.

Routes not pursued to a documented source in this pass, for lack of time rather than absence of tradition: sub-Saharan African, Southeast Asian, Caribbean, and rabbinic/Talmudic material. A Talmud search surfaced general rabbinic attitudes toward wine and intoxication tolerance but no specific hangover-remedy passage, so it was dropped rather than stretched to fit.

## 2. Native vocabulary and conceptual terrain

Each tradition names the problem differently, and the name carries its own theory:

- Chinese: *jiě jiǔ* (解酒), "to release/untie wine" — the formula discussed below is literally "kudzu flower powder that releases intoxication," treating alcohol as something lodged in the body to be loosened, not a toxin to be neutralized [TR1].
- Korean: *haejang* (해장), glossed in the source as "hae" (release/relieve) + "jang" (intestines/viscera) — a soup "to loosen the knot alcohol leaves in the gut" [TR2].
- Sanskrit: *madātyaya* (मदात्यय) — *mada* (intoxication, exhilaration) + *atyaya* (excess, transgression, danger). The compound frames the condition as an excess or transgression of a normally positive state (wine taken properly is described as producing "exhilaration, energy, happiness"), not as a poisoning [TR3].
- Persian/Arabic: *khamr* and *sharâb* for wine itself, discussed by temperament (wine is "generally warm" but this "varies by color, taste, concentration, and age") — the vocabulary is humoral, not toxicological [TR4].
- Russian: *pokhmel'e*, the hangover-day itself, named as a condition with its own remedial dish, not just an absence of a cure [TR5].
- Nahuatl: *octli* (the Aztec term for what is now called pulque), governed by ritual and legal vocabulary (permitted cups, named deities of drink) rather than a recovery vocabulary — the sources found describe regulating intoxication, not treating its aftermath [TR9].

## 3. Major pathways or strains of thought

These six pathways are the researcher's synthesis across the traditions above, not any single tradition's own category system; each tradition's internal logic is described intact within its own entry.

**Humoral/constitutional differentiation.** Ayurveda does not treat madātyaya as one condition: Caraka Samhita differentiates Vata-, Pitta-, and Kapha-dominant intoxication by cause and symptom, and prescribes near-opposite treatments — warming oils and meat broth for Vata, cooling grape juice and sandal paste for Pitta, emesis and heating spices for Kapha (verses 90–109) [TR3]. Persian/Galenic medicine runs a parallel but separate logic, varying wine's effect and remedy by the drinker's temperament and the wine's own qualities [TR4].

**Pattern-formula response, not symptom list.** The Chinese formula Ge Hua Jie Cheng San, from Li Dong-yuan's *Lan Shi Mi Cang* (Jin-Yuan dynasty), targets a named pattern — alcohol injury to the Spleen-Stomach with damp-heat accumulation — with a thirteen-herb formula (kudzu flower as chief herb, plus cardamom, aucklandia, citrus peels, poria, atractylodes, ginseng, and others) built to drain damp and rectify qi together, not a single active ingredient [TR1].

**Restorative hot broth as vernacular response.** Independently across food cultures: Korean haejang-guk (meat, noodles, scallions, historically with cheoncho/Sichuan-pepper powder) [TR2]; Japanese miso soup, often with shijimi clams, believed to restore liver function and aid digestion [TR6]; and the Russian dish recorded by Olearius in 1635 — cold lamb, pepper, and cucumber in vinegar and pickle-brine — served as morning-after food [TR5]. These sit in food culture and tavern life, not formal medical texts.

**Sour/salted/fermented correction.** A recurring instinct toward acid and salt: Russian pickle brine (rassol) drunk directly or as rassolnik soup [TR5]; Japanese umeboshi, salted and souring, believed to aid digestion and ease nausea and "systemic toxicity, including hangovers" [TR6]; and the vinegar-and-Worcestershire prairie oyster of the American plains [TR8]. Three unconnected traditions converge on the same flavor profile.

**Same-substance ("hair of the dog") reasoning.** The oldest attestation found is Bronze Age: a Ugaritic text describes the god ʾIlu treating his own hangover with a salve of dog hair and an unknown plant in olive oil, read by scholars against the "like cures like" principle later named by Pliny [TR7]. The English phrase is attested from 1546 and metaphorically extended to drinking by the 19th century (Brewer's *Dictionary of Phrase and Fable*, 1898); Rabelais used the French equivalent in the 16th century [TR7]. Ayurveda contains an explicit textual cousin of this logic, distinct from the European line: "diseases caused by improper, excessive and deficient drinking of liquor are pacified by consumption of the same in appropriate quantity" (verse 109) [TR3].

**Regulation of the drinking itself, not just its aftermath.** The Aztec sources found are about prevention through limit, not remedy: pulque was hedged with a "fifth cup" threshold said to guarantee bad behavior, restricted mostly to nobles, priests, and the elderly (permitted full intoxication only over roughly age 70), with severe penalties for transgression documented in the Florentine Codex, Codex Mendoza, and the chronicles of Sahagún and Durán [TR9]. This is a different kind of practice than the other five pathways — social and legal rather than culinary or pharmacological — and it does not map onto a "next-day recovery" practice at all.

## 4. Variations, disagreements, and negative space

Six points where the traditions disagree with each other, disagree internally, or where the record runs out:

1. Ayurveda's own texts refuse a single hangover remedy — the Vata/Pitta/Kapha treatments are not variations on a theme but opposite interventions (warming versus cooling) depending on cause. Any modern gloss that reduces madātyaya treatment to one practice erases this differentiation [TR3].

2. Ge Hua Jie Cheng San targets a named organ-system pattern (Spleen-Stomach damp-heat), not "hangover" as such, and is a thirteen-herb combination, not a single-ingredient remedy. The existing Folkprotocol page cites it only as background for its kudzu-flower and Hovenia-dulcis entries — extracting one herb from a differentiated formula built around a different chief action is a real simplification worth flagging, not necessarily a false one [TR1].

3. Persian/Galenic sources show "considerable disagreement" among named physicians (Aghili Khorasani, Arzani, Jorjani, alongside Avicenna) about safe drinking quantities and appropriate remedies, varying by individual temperament — there is no single canonical Unani-adjacent protocol to point to [TR4].

4. Two of the pathways above are in direct tension: "hair of the dog" says re-dose; Aztec cup-limits and the general tenor of the Ayurvedic and Persian material say restrain. Both are textually attested traditions of long standing, not a right answer and a folk error [TR7][TR9][TR3][TR4].

5. Access gap: Donguibogam (1613), Korea's major materia medica text, did not yield a specific hangover-remedy passage in this pass despite searching directly for one. Haejang-guk's documented lineage runs instead through a language-teaching text (*Nogeoldae*) and later poetry and genre painting — food-and-manners history, not the formal medical canon. This may be a search-depth failure rather than a real absence in the text [TR10][TR2].

6. Likely retrofit: Opuntia ficus-indica ("nopal," the herb behind the Folkprotocol page's Tier 2 pre-drinking recommendation) is repeatedly called an "Aztec hangover cure" in commercial and secondary material, but the primary-adjacent sources found here do not support that specific claim. Documented traditional Mexican indications for nopal are diabetes, digestion, wounds, and fatigue generally [TR11]; the actual Aztec alcohol material found concerns legal cup-limits on pulque, not a morning-after cactus remedy [TR9]. This looks like a modern clinical-trial plant (the trial cited on the existing page is European) acquiring a retrofitted indigenous origin story rather than a documented pre-Hispanic hangover lineage — flagged as inference, not established fact, since absence of evidence in this pass is not proof of absence in the wider record.

## 5. Translation problems, access failures, and unanswered questions

The clearest translation flattening is Ge Hua Jie Cheng San being cited in modern secondary literature as background for "kudzu flower" or Hovenia dulcis as isolated active ingredients, when the source formula is a differentiated thirteen-herb pattern-treatment with kudzu flower as only the chief, not sole, herb [TR1]. A second flattening risk sits in madātyaya: English summaries that render it simply as "hangover" or "alcoholism" collapse a term that Caraka Samhita itself splits into at least three constitutional subtypes with opposite treatments [TR3].

Access failures: no specific hangover passage was located in Donguibogam despite it being the obvious place to look for one [TR10]; the Talmud search surfaced general attitudes toward wine tolerance but nothing specific enough to cite as a hangover practice, so nothing was written up rather than stretching a general source; the nopal/Aztec attribution could not be traced to a primary or primary-adjacent source in this pass, which is reported above as a probable retrofit but is an absence-of-evidence finding, not a confirmed negative [TR11][TR9].

Unanswered: whether haejang-guk-type broths have a documented pre-modern medical (as opposed to culinary/tavern) rationale in Korean sources; what the fuller Perso-Islamic disagreement between named physicians actually consisted of beyond the summary in [TR4], which is itself a secondary review rather than the primary texts; and whether African, Southeast Asian, and Caribbean traditions have comparably documented lineages — genuinely unsearched in this pass, not searched-and-absent.
