# Debunking — Testosterone Supplement Myths

## Tribulus Terrestris

The biggest myth in testosterone supplementation. Marketed as a testosterone booster for decades, the evidence says otherwise.

- Multiple RCTs in healthy men show NO testosterone increase
- 8 of 10 studies in the folkprotocol bibliography reported no significant hormonal changes
- The 2 studies showing modest T increases (60-70 ng/dL) were exclusively in hypogonadal subjects
- Does appear to improve erectile function and libido through non-hormonal pathways
- The tribulus materia page already covers this thoroughly

## D-Aspartic Acid (DAA)

- Initial study (Topo 2009) showed a transient 42% increase in testosterone after 12 days
- Follow-up studies (Willoughby 2013, Melville 2015) showed levels return to baseline with continued use
- No sustained testosterone benefit demonstrated in any long-term study
- The "spike and return" pattern suggests the HPG axis compensates

## DHEA (Dehydroepiandrosterone)

- Precursor hormone that can convert to both testosterone and estrogen
- In women and elderly: may modestly increase testosterone
- In young men: no consistent testosterone benefit, and can increase estrogen
- Regulatory status varies — prescription in some countries, OTC supplement in US
- Not a reliable testosterone-raising strategy for most men

## Boron

- Some evidence for a small increase in free testosterone at 6mg/day
- Naghii 2011: 10mg/day for 7 days increased free T by 28% — but very small study
- Mechanism may involve SHBG reduction
- Evidence is marginal and study quality is low
- Not harmful at low doses, but not a primary strategy

## ZMA (Zinc + Magnesium + Vitamin B6)

- The original Brilla & Conte (2000) study showed T increase in football players
- Study has been criticized for methodological issues
- Subsequent research: ZMA has no effect on testosterone in men who are NOT deficient
- Works if you're genuinely zinc-deficient (common in athletes, vegetarians, heavy sweaters)
- Zinc deficiency itself causes low T — supplementation corrects the deficiency, not "boosts" T
- Better framed as "correct your deficiency" than "take a supplement"

## The Category Problem

Clemesha et al. (2020) systematically reviewed 50 testosterone booster supplements:
- 90% of the products made claims about "testosterone support"
- Less than 25% of the ingredients had any supporting data
- Many products contained ingredients with NO human evidence at all
- The gap between marketing and evidence is enormous

The honest framing: a few herbs have modest evidence for normalizing testosterone in men with low-normal levels. Nothing sold as a "testosterone booster" will dramatically increase testosterone in healthy men with normal levels.
